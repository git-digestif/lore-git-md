This is a reply in the ongoing design discussion about adopting an automatic code formatter for Git. Elijah Newren pushes back against Ævar Arnfjörð Bjarmason’s pragmatic concerns, reaffirming his support for the `clang-format` proposal while acknowledging the challenges of transitioning. The exchange centers on two key points: whether the end-state benefits of automated formatting outweigh the transition costs, and whether the proposal unfairly disadvantages contributors on platforms like NonStop.

Elijah argues that the barrier to submitting patches does not increase after the switch to an automatic formatter, since contributors would still only need to match the project’s style—just as they do today. He dismisses the idea that delayed feedback (e.g., from CI) is a significant problem, noting that contributors already deal with similar delays for other issues. On platform compatibility, he counters that the proposal would enable 90% of contributors to catch formatting issues locally, a clear improvement over the current state where no one can. He also downplays Randall S. Becker’s concerns about NonStop’s lack of `clang-format` support, framing it as a non-issue since contributors on such platforms would face the same formatting requirements regardless of whether an automatic tool exists.

The reply also touches on the difficulty of writing a versatile code formatter, reinforcing Elijah’s preference for compiler-associated tools like `clang-format` over custom solutions. While he concedes that switching to an automatic formatter is a massive effort—likely requiring style adjustments and exclusions for generated or "untouchable" code—he sees the end result as worth the cost, even for contributors who do not use the tool themselves. His position remains that if others are willing to do the work, the project should green-light the effort, though he is not volunteering to lead it.

The discussion remains polarized: Elijah frames the proposal as a net win with manageable trade-offs, while Ævar’s concerns about practical friction (e.g., delayed feedback, enforcement gates, and debates over formatting overrides) go unaddressed. Randall’s pluggable system counterproposal is still not engaged with by other participants. The thread’s trajectory hinges on whether the community views the transition costs as a one-time hurdle or a dealbreaker.
