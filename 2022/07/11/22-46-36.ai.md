This is a reply in the ongoing design discussion about adopting an automatic code formatter for Git, specifically addressing the question of whether to enforce a line-length limit in `.clang-format`. Junio C Hamano responds to brian m. carlson’s suggestion to either stick with Git’s traditional 80-character limit or adopt a longer limit like 132 characters.

Junio’s reply is brief but introduces a nuanced perspective on line-wrapping conventions. He expresses openness to a longer line limit (e.g., 132 characters) as long as contributors do not abuse it to produce "unreadable mess" by consistently writing code that stretches to the maximum width. His concern is not just about the numeric limit but about the *quality* of line breaks—Git’s documented style allows flexibility around the 80-column guideline, encouraging developers to wrap lines at "good" places (e.g., after operators or logical breaks) even if it slightly exceeds 80 columns. He warns against using automation as an excuse to ignore readability or to dismiss reviewer feedback on line-wrapping issues that cannot be fixed mechanically. The tone is cautionary rather than prescriptive, emphasizing that automation should not replace human judgment.

This reply adds a new dimension to the discussion: whether `clang-format` can replicate Git’s flexible, context-aware line-wrapping conventions. Junio’s concern suggests that a rigid `ColumnLimit` rule might not fully align with Git’s style, even if set to 80 or 132 characters. Unlike brian, who frames the question as a binary choice between two limits, Junio highlights the importance of *how* lines are wrapped, not just their length. His position does not reject automation outright but sets a high bar for its ability to handle nuanced formatting. The reply does not propose a solution (e.g., tweaking `.clang-format` rules to better match Git’s conventions) or engage with the broader debate about Randall’s pluggable formatter counterproposal. It is narrowly focused on the immediate question of line-length enforcement and its implications for code readability.
