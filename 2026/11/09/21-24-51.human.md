This is Junio C Hamano’s latest reply in the ongoing thread about Farid Zakaria’s patch series fixing race conditions in the Git credential-cache daemon. The series aims to address two issues: a socket-stealing race during concurrent daemon starts and a pre-existing permission-handling bug in socket directory creation. While the core fixes in patches 2 and 3 are technically sound and have been confirmed by reviewers, Junio has raised an unresolved architectural concern about patch 3’s use of both `unix_ss_*` and `tempfile` APIs, which he argues is unsafe and inconsistent with precedent in `fsmonitor--daemon.c`.

Junio’s email pushes back sharply on Farid’s earlier response, which framed the contribution as a "best-effort fix" and deferred further discussion. Junio clarifies that when a reviewer points out a potential issue, the author’s job is not to question whether addressing it is "necessary" but to either justify why the concern is unfounded or revise the patch. Farid’s phrasing—"I can address it if we think it's necessary"—struck Junio as dismissive, and he makes it clear that such responses do not advance the discussion. The tone is firm but not personal; Junio is emphasizing the collaborative expectation that contributors engage substantively with review feedback rather than treating it as optional.

The architectural concern remains the blocker for the series. Junio has previously argued that mixing `unix_ss_*` and `tempfile` APIs risks socket removal by a dying daemon that no longer owns it, and he pointed to `fsmonitor--daemon.c` as a model for using `unix_ss_create()` without `tempfile` registration. Farid’s earlier claim—that the race might only occur during Git upgrades—was rejected by Junio, who countered that any serious installation would have a babysitter process to respawn dead daemons, making the race a persistent issue. This reply does not re-litigate that point but instead focuses on the process: Junio is signaling that the series will not move forward until the architectural question is resolved, either through a convincing justification or a revised implementation.

For a busy reader, this email is a clear signal that the series is stuck. The technical fixes are otherwise solid, but the unresolved design issue is a showstopper. Junio’s message suggests that Farid needs to either engage with the concern or risk the series being dropped. The tone also hints at broader project norms: Git development expects contributors to take review feedback seriously, especially when it comes from experienced maintainers. If Farid does not provide a substantive response, the next step may be for Junio or another reviewer to propose a revised implementation themselves.
