This is a review exchange in the thread introducing `checkout.orphanedCommitWarning`, a new configuration knob that lets users disable the expensive reachability check Git performs when leaving a detached HEAD. The patch is part of Wyatt Holden’s three-patch series aimed at addressing a performance bottleneck in large mono-repos, where the check can consume over 90% of checkout time.

Junio C Hamano opens with curiosity about what makes the orphaned-commit check so costly, asking whether the expense comes from the number of branches, the length of the commit chain being left behind, or something else. He wonders if the check could be optimized rather than simply disabled. Wyatt responds that the exact breakdown isn’t known—stack dumps showed the main thread stuck in `orphaned_commit_warning()` enumerating loose refs and opening ref files, but it’s unclear how much of the delay came from enumeration versus history traversal. Junio acknowledges the uncertainty but doesn’t press further, calling the opt-out approach "sensible."

The review then turns to the design choice to omit a dedicated command-line option for the new setting. Junio questions whether `--quiet` (`-q`) is a sufficient alternative for human users, noting that it suppresses more than just the orphaned-commit warning—including the "Previous HEAD position was" message and any "local changes" output. Wyatt’s patch preserves that message when the warning is disabled, but Junio’s point is that users who want to skip the check might still want other checkout feedback. The implication is that a command-line flag (e.g., `--no-orphaned-commit-warning`) could be more precise than `--quiet`.

The rest of the exchange is straightforward. Junio approves the Trace2 region added around `orphaned_commit_warning()` to measure its cost and verify the setting works, and he notes the clean placement of the `trace2_region_leave` call at the end of the function. He also observes that the loop in `suggest_reattach()` (called by `orphaned_commit_warning()`) keeps counting commits even after it stops displaying them, suggesting an alternative: a configuration to limit how many commits are checked rather than disabling the warning entirely. This hints at a possible future direction but doesn’t block the current patch.

The review is substantive, engaging with both the performance trade-offs and the user-interface design. Junio’s questions are probing but not dismissive, and the discussion leaves room for follow-up work without derailing the immediate goal. The patch itself remains well-motivated and technically sound, with clear evidence of a real-world performance issue. The main open question is whether the lack of a command-line option will prove inconvenient for human users.
