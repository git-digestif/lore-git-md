This is a **workflow policy update** for Git’s integration pipeline, specifically refining how the `seen` branch handles stagnant topics. Junio adopts Phillip Wood’s suggestion to explicitly frame ejection from `seen` as a reversible, routine event rather than a final rejection. The change is purely about messaging and contributor psychology, with no technical or mechanical adjustments to the policy itself.

The new heading for the "Discarded" section in Junio’s "What's cooking" reports will now read: *"Many of these topics were picked up prematurely but did not prove ready for inclusion, and will be removed. They can be resubmit if they gained enough interest and showed good fit to the system in the future, unless noted otherwise."* This wording aims to reduce contributor anxiety by emphasizing that ejection is temporary and contingent on renewed engagement, not a judgment on the patch’s merit. The phrasing also leaves room for exceptions (e.g., topics marked as fundamentally flawed), preserving maintainer discretion.

This update directly addresses the social dimension of the earlier policy discussion, where Phillip highlighted the risk of demotivating contributors by framing ejection as a low-stakes, reversible action. By explicitly stating that topics can be re-queued, Junio aligns the policy’s communication with its intent: to manage the `seen` branch as a staging area for active review, not a graveyard for abandoned work. The change does not alter the four-week expiration period or the requirement for **trusted reviewer endorsement** to enter `seen`, but it softens the perceived harshness of ejection.

The broader policy context remains unchanged. Topics in `seen` will still expire after four weeks without positive feedback, and Junio continues to disclaim responsibility for reviewing every patch himself. The strict intake criterion (requiring endorsement from a trusted reviewer) is now the de facto standard, though Junio has not formally committed to it. This update focuses solely on how the policy is presented to contributors, not its mechanics or enforcement.

Key implications for future discussion:
- **Contributor experience**: The new wording may encourage authors of ejected topics to re-submit if they address feedback or attract new reviewers, rather than interpreting ejection as a permanent setback.
- **Reviewer behavior**: The policy’s reversibility could reduce pressure on reviewers to provide superficial approvals to prevent ejection, as contributors now have a clearer path to re-queuing.
- **Documentation gaps**: The update does not address Phillip’s earlier suggestion to document the reversibility policy in `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` or elsewhere, leaving this as an open question. The "Discarded" section in "What's cooking" reports will now serve as the primary source of this guidance.
- **Policy evolution**: This change reflects a shift toward balancing mechanical rigor (e.g., expiration periods) with social considerations (e.g., contributor morale). Future iterations may explore additional ways to signal reversibility, such as automated reminders for ejected topics or clearer criteria for re-queuing.

The update is a small but meaningful step in refining Git’s integration pipeline, demonstrating how policy design must account for both technical and human factors. It does not resolve the core tension between reviewer convenience and contributor expectations but provides a framework for managing it more transparently.
