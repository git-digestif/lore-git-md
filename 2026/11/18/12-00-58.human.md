This is a substantive review reply to patch 3/5 of Ravi Mistry's HTTP retry series, challenging the design decision to make retryable status codes user-configurable. Patrick Steinhardt argues against the `http.retryStatusCodes` approach on ecosystem grounds, suggesting that pushing the configuration burden onto every Git user risks fragmenting behavior across providers rather than pressuring server operators to standardize on a single error code (presumably 429). He frames the configurable option as a capitulation that could lead to providers telling users they are "holding it wrong," and instead proposes that Git's new retry capability should serve as leverage to nudge hosting backends toward fixing their infrastructure so that clients "just work" without configuration.

The comment engages at the product-design level rather than the code level, contrasting with the more mechanical feedback Patrick offered on patches 1/5 and 2/5 (retry counter types, commit organization). Here he questions whether the feature should exist in its current opt-in form at all, representing a genuine philosophical fork in the road: configurability for immediate practical relief versus ecosystem pressure for long-term consistency. The tone is constructive but clearly skeptical of the fragmentation risk inherent in letting users decide which 5xx codes to retry.

Within the broader thread, this reply arrives after the technical details of v3 have largely settled (including the 10-second default backoff compromise), shifting the remaining discussion toward whether the configurable retry codes should remain opt-in or be replaced by a hardcoded standard. Ravi Mistry's original patch motivation -- handling transient 502/503/504 responses from load balancers during load shedding -- is precisely what Patrick is pushing back on, arguing those servers should be fixed rather than accommodated. The series now awaits a response to this design challenge alongside Junio's pending decision on patch organization.
