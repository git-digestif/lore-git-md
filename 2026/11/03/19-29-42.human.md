This is a **workflow policy discussion** about Git’s integration branches, specifically how aggressively unreviewed or under-reviewed topics should be ejected from the `seen` branch. Junio responds to Patrick Steinhardt’s concerns about the trade-offs between early integration testing and the risk of cluttering `seen` with low-quality patches.

Junio clarifies his stance on when a topic should enter `seen`: not just because it exists, but because it has attracted enough positive attention to justify consuming project resources. He argues that `seen` should not be a dumping ground for every patch—only those with at least one substantive review from a trusted contributor indicating the change has potential. This aligns with his earlier proposal to eject stagnant topics after four weeks, but here he emphasizes the *intake* criteria rather than the ejection timeline.

The key tension is between two goals: giving reviewers early access to patches (so they can test interactions with other in-flight work) and avoiding premature promotion of under-scrutinized changes. Junio leans toward stricter intake, reasoning that uninspired or forgettable patches waste maintainer and reviewer bandwidth. He dismisses the idea that `seen` should include every patch just to test interactions, arguing that authors should do their homework first—ensuring their work meshes with existing topics—before expecting the project to invest resources in it.

Patrick’s middle-ground suggestion—that a single favorable review (even if not a full `Reviewed-by`) should suffice for `seen` inclusion—gets a lukewarm response. Junio agrees that perfection shouldn’t be required upfront, but he insists the bar must be higher than a random drive-by comment. The implication is that the reviewer’s credibility and the depth of their feedback matter more than the mere existence of a review.

This exchange doesn’t resolve the policy, but it sharpens the debate. Junio’s position suggests he’s leaning toward stricter intake, while Patrick’s concern about delayed integration testing remains unaddressed. The discussion is now framed around trust in reviewers and the cost of prematurely consuming project resources, rather than just mechanical ejection timelines. For readers, this signals that the `seen` branch’s role may tighten, with contributors needing to secure at least one credible review before their work is queued.
