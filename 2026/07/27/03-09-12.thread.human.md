This thread is Junio Hamano’s periodic "What's cooking in git.git" report from July 2026, which has grown into a broader discussion about Git’s integration pipeline, review quality, and the design of two specific topics: `hn/history-squash` and `hn/branch-delete-merged`. The thread has also become the venue for a new maintainer policy on the `seen` branch, now implemented and active.

The core of the thread is Junio’s integration report, which lists all in-flight topics across his branches (`seen`, `next`, `master`). Two topics drew particular scrutiny: `hn/history-squash` was ejected from `next` after Phillip Wood’s review exposed fundamental flaws in its reachability logic and user experience, while `hn/branch-delete-merged` remains in `next` but faces unresolved design questions about upstream push-check logic and merged-hierarchy behavior. Phillip Wood is actively developing fixups for `hn/history-squash`, but the command’s message-handling behavior remains contentious, with four competing proposals now converging on a simpler, always-open-editor approach.

The most significant development is Junio’s new policy for the `seen` branch, now in effect. Topics will not graduate to `next` without substantive review, will expire after four weeks without positive feedback, and Junio has disclaimed responsibility for reviewing every patch himself. This policy aims to balance reviewer productivity with quality control, addressing concerns raised by Phillip Wood and Matt Hunter about premature promotion of under-scrutinized patches. Patrick Steinhardt endorsed the four-week expiration period, aligning it with the existing "Waiting for response" timeout, and suggested documenting the previously undocumented "Will merge to 'next'?" state to reduce confusion for newcomers.

The thread has also surfaced systemic concerns about review quality and the tension between velocity and correctness. Matt Hunter reflected on his earlier surface-level review of `hn/history-squash`, acknowledging the weight his feedback carried and raising procedural questions about how reviewers should signal engagement depth. Junio’s policy does not directly address these gaps but may indirectly encourage deeper engagement by preventing premature promotion. The long-term impact of the new `seen` policy remains to be seen, particularly whether it will improve review velocity or merely shift the bottleneck from `next` to `seen`. Key open questions include how contributors will adapt to the need for proactive engagement, how the community will define "substantive review," and whether the policy will discourage reviewers by adding friction to the testing process.
