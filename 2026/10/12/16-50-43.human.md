This is a **maintainer's architectural review** of Harald Nordgren’s patch series to extend `git branch --delete-merged` to detect squash-merged and rebase-merged branches. Junio C Hamano responds to Phillip Wood’s call for clarity on the algorithmic trade-offs by proposing a **fundamental reorganization** of the series.

Junio agrees with Phillip that the trade-offs between different detection methods (tree-based diffing, patch-ids, test merges) need to be clearly understood before further work proceeds. He then suggests splitting the implementation into **two distinct patches**: one for **rebase-and-merge detection** (handling one-to-one rebases) and another for **squash-merge detection** (handling many-to-one merges). The two workflows, he argues, require fundamentally different algorithms and should not share a unified implementation.

For rebase detection, Junio recommends reusing the **commit-matching logic from `git range-diff`**, which considers log message similarity and authorship information alongside patch content. This approach would be more robust than relying solely on patch-ids, especially for rebases where commit messages are preserved. He is skeptical that rebase detection can be efficiently implemented as a subset of squash-merge detection, citing performance concerns.

On the user interface front, Junio questions whether a single `--delete-merged` flag that enables all detection variants by default—with an opt-out like `--no-squashed`—is the right long-term design. He anticipates future workflows (e.g., selective cherry-picks) that might require additional detection methods, and a monolithic flag could become unwieldy. This suggests the series may need to revisit its CLI design to accommodate extensibility.

The email advances the thread by **shifting the focus from algorithmic details to architectural structure**. Junio’s proposal to split the series into two patches—one for rebase detection (using `git range-diff` logic) and another for squash detection—addresses his earlier concern that the two workflows are too distinct to share a unified implementation. This reorganization would also make it easier to evaluate the trade-offs of each approach independently. The discussion now hinges on whether the author will adopt this split and how the CLI will evolve to handle future detection methods.
