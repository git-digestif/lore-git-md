This is an RFC for a new tool, `git-brebase`, proposed by Alejandro Colomar as a more powerful alternative to `git-imerge`. The tool aims to simplify complex rebases, particularly those involving merge commits or nested branch structures, by using a bisection-based approach to identify and resolve conflicts incrementally.

The core idea is to rebase only up to the first conflicting commit in the target branch, rather than attempting the entire rebase at once. This allows users to resolve conflicts step-by-step, with optional pre- and post-rebase hooks (`--pre-exec` and `--post-exec`) to skip broken commits or verify semantic correctness. The tool also supports `--first-parent` for bisecting, making it easier to handle merge-heavy histories. Unlike `git-imerge`, which builds a 2D matrix of conflict resolutions, `git-brebase` focuses on rebasing and relies on repeated invocations to progress toward the final state, keeping the intermediate history lean and easier to inspect.

Alejandro highlights several advantages over `git-imerge`, including better support for rebasing trees of branches (e.g., nested merge commits) and the ability to interrupt and resume the process. The tool is implemented as a 164-line shell script that wraps `git-bisect` and `git-rebase`, passing most flags transparently to the latter. However, the author acknowledges limitations, such as the lack of native merge-commit support (though a workaround is suggested) and rudimentary option parsing (e.g., no support for `--foo bar` syntax).

The proposal is well-motivated, with clear use cases for complex rebases, but it’s still early-stage. The tool’s design—particularly its reliance on repeated invocations and external hooks—may appeal to power users but could be less intuitive for casual Git users. The author seeks feedback on whether this should be a standalone command or integrated into `git-rebase` as a new flag, noting that the specialized hooks and bisect dependency might make the latter awkward. Junio Hamano, a fan of `git-imerge`, is explicitly invited to weigh in, which could shape the discussion. For now, this looks like a promising experiment worth watching, especially for teams dealing with intricate branch topologies.
