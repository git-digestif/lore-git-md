This is the latest installment in the long-running thread about formalizing a community voting process for Git. Emily Shaffer follows up on her earlier patch proposing `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` with a status update and two key open questions: which voting platform to use, and how to define voter eligibility.

The patch itself is unchanged from the previous version, but the discussion has narrowed to implementation details. On platforms, Emily leans toward CIVS (Condorcet Internet Voting Service) because it is hosted, user-friendly, and already used by other projects. She dismisses GitHub polls on principle—Git has never depended on a forge—and notes that Debian’s *devotee* would require self-hosting. No one has yet volunteered to set up or maintain a voting system, so the choice may hinge on which option requires the least infrastructure work.

On eligibility, Emily acknowledges the community’s near-universal pushback against her initial thresholds (two code reviews or one merged commit). The thread has since coalesced around three quantitative criteria: total commits, total topics, or commits within a recent time window. Peff provided empirical data showing how many contributors would qualify under various thresholds and windows; Junio Hamano prefers counting topics for simplicity. Emily herself is now on vacation and will not respond until mid-November, so the thread is effectively paused until then.

The patch is well-structured and clearly motivated, but the unresolved questions—especially voter eligibility—are critical. Until those are settled, the process cannot be used for the AI contribution policy vote it was designed to enable. If the project can agree on a platform and a threshold, this looks like a straightforward documentation addition that will serve as a template for future contentious decisions.
