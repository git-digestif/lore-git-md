This email is the latest installment in the ongoing discussion about Git’s proposed **community voting process**, a governance mechanism designed to resolve contentious decisions like the AI contribution policy. Emily Shaffer follows up on her earlier patch with a summary of planned changes and a focused discussion of two remaining open questions: **which voting platform to use** and **how to limit voter eligibility**.

The email is **not a new patch** but a status update and call for consensus on unresolved details. Emily’s planned changes—clarifying that votes must be secret, specifying that only the most recent proposal version will be voted on, and requiring votes to reference a commit ID rather than a mailing-list subject—are uncontroversial and address earlier feedback. The real debate centers on the two open questions.

On **voting platforms**, Emily narrows the field to two Condorcet-based systems: **CIVS** (hosted service, easier to set up) and **devotee** (Debian’s system, would require self-hosting). She leans toward CIVS for its simplicity and neutrality, noting that the maintainer is not affiliated with a major tech company or forge. Brian m. carlson’s earlier opposition to GitHub-based voting is cited as a constraint, reinforcing Git’s historical neutrality toward forges. No one has proposed a third option, so the choice is effectively between convenience (CIVS) and alignment with Debian’s well-regarded process (devotee).

The **voter eligibility** debate is more complex. Emily summarizes three proposed criteria: total number of topics merged, total number of commits merged, or number of commits merged within a recent time window (e.g., the past N years). Jeff King’s earlier email provided empirical data showing how many contributors would qualify under different thresholds (e.g., 50 commits in 2 years vs. 5 commits in 5 years), while Junio C Hamano has suggested counting topics rather than commits for simplicity. Emily does not endorse a specific approach but signals that the current proposal (2+ code reviews or any commit merged to `next`) is too permissive and needs refinement.

The email’s tone is **pragmatic and inclusive**, acknowledging the trade-offs in both platform choice and eligibility criteria. Emily’s upcoming vacation means the thread will pause for at least two weeks, giving the community time to weigh in on these final details. The unresolved questions are **not technical** but **governance design**: how to balance inclusivity, simplicity, and resistance to manipulation. The discussion so far suggests a preference for **moderate thresholds** (e.g., 25–50 commits) and **time-limited activity windows** (e.g., 2–3 years) to ensure voters are engaged but not overly restrictive.

Key technical details for future reference:
- **Files**: `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` (the core process doc) and a reference in `Documentation/DecisionMaking.adoc`.
- **Voting mechanism**: Condorcet-based (CIVS or devotee), with no serious consideration of GitHub polls.
- **Eligibility criteria**: Likely to settle on a hybrid of commit count (e.g., 25–50) and recent activity (e.g., 2–3 years), though the exact formula remains open.
- **Next steps**: Emily will reroll the patch after November 12, incorporating consensus on the platform and eligibility rules. The AI contribution policy vote will depend on this process being finalized.
