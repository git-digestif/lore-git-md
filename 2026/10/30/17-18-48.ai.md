This is a governance discussion about the Git project’s proposed community voting process. Junio C Hamano responds to Emily Shaffer’s earlier email, where she expressed concern about being excluded from voting due to her reduced capacity for code contributions in recent years. Junio broadens the scope of what counts as a meaningful contribution, arguing that documentation, reviews, and even informal support (e.g., on IRC or Discord) should be considered when determining voter eligibility.

The email does not propose a specific technical solution or threshold but instead advocates for a more inclusive definition of contribution that goes beyond merged commits. Junio’s stance aligns with Emily’s call for qualitative inclusivity, particularly for contributors with deep but non-code involvement in the project. However, it also raises practical challenges: how to fairly and transparently measure non-code contributions (e.g., reviews, mentorship, or community support) without introducing subjective bias or administrative overhead. This tension—between inclusivity and scriptability—remains unresolved, though Junio’s framing suggests the project may need to expand its criteria beyond the current quantitative metrics (e.g., commit counts or patch series).

The discussion is still in the design phase, with no code or documentation changes yet. Future follow-ups will likely explore how to operationalize Junio’s suggestion, such as whether to count substantive reviews (as Phillip Wood proposed earlier), track mailing-list or IRC activity, or rely on self-reported contributions. The thread’s unresolved questions now include:
- How to define and measure non-code contributions (e.g., reviews, documentation, community support) in a way that is both fair and automatable?
- Whether to adopt a hybrid model (e.g., Phillip Wood’s proposal requiring both a track record *and* recent activity) or a more flexible approach that accommodates contributors like Emily.
- How to balance the need for inclusivity with the risk of "drive-by" voting or administrative complexity, especially for contentious decisions like the AI contribution policy.
