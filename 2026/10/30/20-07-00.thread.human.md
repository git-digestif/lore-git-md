This thread is about fixing long-standing bugs in `git log --follow`—a feature that tracks file renames across history—but the fixes have introduced a significant performance regression. Originally, Miklos Vajna identified that `--follow` became about 14 times slower after a 2026 commit (304812ed33) when processing merges, and his patches aimed to restore performance while also fixing two correctness issues: `-m --follow` losing history from non-first parents and `--follow` missing edits on dropped sides of merges. Justin Tobler took over the series, posting v5, which reduced the regression to about 1.6 times slower than the pre-2026 baseline (from ~23s to ~39s) while addressing reviewer concerns about file copies and unrelated histories.

The series is now under review, with no maintainer decision yet. The key change in v5 was simplifying the logic to first identify a parent where the tracked path is unchanged before diffing other parents, which resolved Patrick Steinhardt’s earlier correctness concerns. However, the remaining performance gap has become a blocker for GitLab’s server-side use cases. In the latest email, Patrick—who had previously focused on technical correctness—now shifts his position, explicitly supporting Justin’s proposal to make `--follow` opt-in via a new flag or even revert the 2026 commit entirely. This marks a turning point: the discussion has moved from "how to fix this" to "should this be fixed at all?" given the trade-off between correctness and performance.

The thread’s open questions now center on user-facing behavior rather than implementation. Should the feature be disabled by default (via a toggle), reverted to its pre-2026 state (risking the original bugs), or further optimized? Patrick’s offer to contribute a default-disabled toggle suggests this is a deployment blocker for GitLab, not just a technical debate. The outcome may require input from the maintainer or Project Leadership Committee, as it touches on broader questions about Git’s default behavior and performance expectations.
