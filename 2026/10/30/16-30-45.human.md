This is a governance documentation patch that formalizes a voting process for the Git project. Emily Shaffer responds to Junio C Hamano’s feedback on her proposal to define how contentious policy decisions—like the ongoing AI contributions debate—can be resolved when consensus fails. The patch itself is documentation-only, adding `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` and updating `Documentation/DecisionMaking.adoc`, with no code changes.

The key update here clarifies how votes will handle multiple competing proposals. Emily explains that the process is designed for scenarios with *N* options (e.g., "Yes/No" or several distinct policy drafts), not just binary choices. She cites ranked-choice voting as a likely mechanism, referencing tools like CIVS and devotee, which would allow voters to express preferences across proposals rather than just picking one. This addresses Junio’s earlier question about whether outcomes are determined by plurality or proportional support.

On eligibility, Emily acknowledges Junio’s preference for a stricter standard—requiring contributors to have authored *two or more topics in released versions*—but notes that Peff’s alternative (a matrix of commit count and recency) also gained traction. She expresses personal hesitation about time-bound filters (e.g., "contributions in the last *X* releases"), fearing they might exclude invested but less active contributors like herself. The trade-off between inclusivity and perceived legitimacy remains unresolved, with no clear consensus yet on which metric the community will adopt.

The patch is well-motivated and pragmatic, framing voting as a last-resort tool for policy stalemates while explicitly excluding technical decisions. Junio’s feedback has refined the proposal, particularly around versioning (using Git OIDs to pin proposal texts) and eligibility, but the core debate—whether Git should adopt formal voting at all—persists. Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s ideological critique (that voting favors corporate interests) and Justin Tobler’s warning about eligibility bias toward corporate-affiliated contributors are still live issues, though the emerging consensus on secret ballots may mitigate some concerns.

This is worth watching for anyone following Git’s governance evolution, especially given its direct ties to the AI contributions debate. The next steps will likely involve settling on eligibility criteria and vote-counting mechanics, both of which could spark further discussion. The patch itself is uncontroversial in structure, but the principles it encodes are far from settled.
