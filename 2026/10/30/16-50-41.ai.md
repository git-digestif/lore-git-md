This is a governance discussion about the Git project's proposed community voting process, specifically refining the eligibility criteria for voters. Emily Shaffer responds to Patrick Steinhardt's framing of the threshold as a trade-off between long-term contributors ("oldtimers") and newcomers, introducing a nuanced perspective on inclusivity and the value of past contributors.

Emily agrees with Patrick that the *shape* of the voting population matters more than the raw number of voters, noting that modern voting platforms can easily handle hundreds of participants. She pushes back gently on the idea that inactive contributors should be excluded, arguing that some former maintainers or deeply involved contributors—even if no longer active—may still have valuable insights, especially on high-stakes decisions like the AI contribution policy. Her personal example (being unable to contribute code recently due to her role at Google but still wanting a voice) adds a human dimension to the debate, highlighting the tension between strict activity-based eligibility and the project’s long-term community bonds.

The email does not propose a specific threshold or time window but reinforces the need to consider qualitative factors beyond Peff’s quantitative data. Key unresolved questions remain: whether to prioritize recent activity (e.g., "any commit in the last 2 years") over all-time contributions, how to handle edge cases like former maintainers, and whether the hybrid model proposed by Phillip Wood (requiring both a track record *and* recent activity) could strike the right balance. The thread is still in the design phase, with no code or documentation changes yet. Future discussion will likely focus on reconciling Patrick’s "middle ground" (e.g., 25–30 commits with a 2–3 year window) with Emily’s call for broader inclusivity, especially for contributors with deep but dormant ties to the project.
