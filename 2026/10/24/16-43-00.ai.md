This is a **substantive review reply** in the ongoing debate over Harald Nordgren’s patch to make `git status` compare against both `@{upstream}` and `@{push}` by default. Junio responds directly to D. Ben Knoble’s user-centric argument, refining his earlier critique by distinguishing between the *nature* of changes users encounter in `@{upstream}` versus `@{push}`.

Junio concedes that users may not always remember the state of their `@{push}` branch but argues that, unlike `@{upstream}`, the changes there are *self-authored*—meaning users will recognize their own work when they see it. This undercuts Knoble’s claim that monitoring `@{push}` is necessary to answer questions like *"Have I rebased lately?"* or *"Do I care about the differences?"*. Junio’s position is that while `@{upstream}` may introduce "surprises" (unexpected commits from others), `@{push}` is effectively a mirror of the user’s own actions, making its divergence less critical to monitor by default. The implication is that the patch’s justification—helping users track divergence in triangular workflows—is weaker for `@{push}` than for `@{upstream}`.

The reply does not address Knoble’s broader point about workflow friction (e.g., his personal tooling for checking divergence) or the cultural debate about Git’s defaults. Instead, it narrows the discussion to a technical distinction: whether `@{push}` divergence is inherently less important than `@{upstream}` divergence. This could shift the burden back to Harald or Knoble to either (1) demonstrate that `@{push}` divergence *is* frequently surprising or disruptive in practice, or (2) accept Junio’s framing and revise the patch to focus solely on `@{upstream}` (which would contradict the original goal). The thread remains **open**, with the philosophical and pragmatic debate unresolved.
