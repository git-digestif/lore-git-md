This is the latest exchange in the ongoing documentation effort to introduce `gitmergeconflicts(7)`, a new man page consolidating Git’s merge-conflict resolution guidance. The thread is now in its third iteration (v3), and this email captures Junio C Hamano’s final review feedback on the first patch of the v2 series, which adds the new guide.

Junio’s comments here are **surface-level but important for consistency and clarity**. He pushes for more precise language in several places: replacing "configuration option" with the project-standard "configuration variable", removing informal contractions like "Here's" and "there's", and clarifying the explanation of `git diff AUTO_MERGE` so readers understand what it represents before first use. He also suggests mentioning the `--left-right` option for `git log --merge -p` to help users distinguish which side (ours/theirs) each commit came from, though Julia Evans expresses hesitation about introducing another directional term ("left"/"right") alongside the already-confusing "ours"/"theirs".

The most substantive discussion revolves around how to frame the user’s choices when a merge conflict occurs. Junio argues the current phrasing ("the most common" ways to handle conflicts) is vague and proposes a clearer, more structured explanation: for each conflicted step in a multi-step operation (like rebase), the user can either resolve the conflict and continue, or skip the step entirely. He notes that `--abort` (which cancels the entire operation) should be presented separately, as it’s a distinct choice. Julia agrees the current wording is weak and plans to revise it, though she also wants to acknowledge that even successful merges can produce broken code—a point Junio confirms is a general truth, not specific to Git.

Junio also endorses Julia’s plan to deprecate `zdiff3` in the guide, calling it a "failed experiment" that reduces `diff3`’s usefulness. His suggested phrasing ("zdiff3 was an alternative to diff3 that makes the merge conflict shorter by introducing more ambiguity") is concise and aligns with the project’s preference for `diff3`.

The email concludes with a historical note: Junio traces a **16-year-old bug** in `git-merge.adoc` (references to non-existent steps "2" and "3") to a 2010 commit that simplified the merge documentation but left outdated instructions intact. Julia’s second patch in the series (v2 2/6) already fixes this, and Junio approves the fix.

Overall, this exchange is **a final polish pass**—no structural changes are needed, and the series remains on track for merging after v3. The feedback is actionable and focused on making the guide more precise and user-friendly. Julia’s responses are responsive and collaborative, addressing every point raised. The new `gitmergeconflicts(7)` man page is well-motivated, thoroughly reviewed, and fills a clear gap in Git’s documentation by centralizing scattered advice on merge conflicts. For readers following Git development, this is a **low-risk, high-value documentation improvement** that’s likely to ship in the next release.
