This is a substantive review of the community voting process patch, focusing on governance design and eligibility criteria. Brian m. Carlson engages directly with Emily Shaffer’s proposal, raising concerns about the threshold for voting eligibility and the project’s tooling neutrality.

Brian argues that the current eligibility criteria—merged commits or two code reviews—are too permissive and could incentivize "drive-by" contributions solely to influence votes. He suggests a higher threshold to ensure voters are "serious contributors" but does not propose a specific number, leaving that as an open question. His critique is grounded in broader governance patterns (e.g., Debian’s tighter access controls) and the risk of gaming the system, though he stops short of advocating for a core-team model.

On tooling, Brian opposes GitHub-based voting, reinforcing Git’s historical neutrality toward forges. He endorses Debian’s *devotee* as a well-regarded system but clarifies he is not volunteering to maintain it. His preference aligns with Emily’s inclination toward email-based systems but adds weight to the argument against GitHub polls.

The review is narrowly scoped to two issues—eligibility and tooling—but both are critical to the process’s legitimacy. Brian’s points are likely to resonate with contributors wary of procedural loopholes or platform bias, though his lack of concrete alternatives (e.g., a specific eligibility threshold) leaves the discussion open for further refinement. Phillip Wood’s earlier review already addressed some of these concerns (e.g., counting patch series rather than individual commits), but Brian’s emphasis on "seriousness" introduces a qualitative dimension that may require subjective judgment or additional criteria.
