This is a substantive review of Emily Shaffer’s community voting process patch, focusing on the eligibility criteria for voting. Phillip Wood engages thoughtfully with the trade-offs Emily outlined—balancing inclusivity, resistance to manipulation ("dogpiling"), and practicality of implementation.

Phillip endorses the principle of restricting eligibility to contributions made on the mailing list (where Git development happens) but raises practical concerns about how to count reviews: should a simple "LGTM" qualify, or must the review be more substantive? He also suggests counting *patch series* rather than individual patches (to avoid inflating counts for large mechanical changes) and proposes measuring contributions by when they reached `master` (not `next`), which is more stable and scriptable. For non-code contributions like bug reports, he asks whether they should count at all, signaling that the current patch’s focus on code/review contributions may be too narrow.

On dogpiling, Phillip proposes two concrete refinements: (1) require contributions to predate the *first draft* of a proposal by at least a month (not just the voting kickoff), to prevent last-minute drive-by contributions; and (2) limit eligibility to contributors active in the past two years, ensuring voters are current participants who will live with the decision’s consequences. These suggestions tighten the guardrails without sacrificing inclusivity.

He also notes the difficulty of scripting eligibility checks, especially for non-code contributions, and humorously (but pointedly) rejects the idea of using an LLM to classify mailing-list activity—highlighting the tension between automation and the AI policy vote this process is designed to resolve.

The review is constructive and detail-oriented, focusing on the *how* of eligibility rather than the *why* of the voting process itself. It advances the discussion by surfacing edge cases (bug reports, inactive contributors) and proposing specific thresholds (one-month lead time, two-year activity window) that could make the process more robust. No objections to the patch’s core premise are raised; this is a refinement, not a rejection.
