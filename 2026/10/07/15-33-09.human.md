This is a substantive follow-up in the ongoing discussion about Git’s ZIP timestamp handling and `git fast-import` date validation. René Scharfe responds to Matthew E. Luallen’s real-world tool observations and policy proposals with a clear preference for strict correctness over tool compatibility.

René dismisses Python’s `ZipInfo.date_time` misreporting as irrelevant to Git’s design, noting that Python’s ZIP parser lacks support for the UNIX timestamp extension (0x000d) and thus has no reason to handle it. This reinforces his earlier stance that Git should not bend its behavior to accommodate tools that ignore standard extensions. He also downplays the practical impact of the 2106-to-1970 wrap in UnZip’s update mode, framing it as a minor quirk rather than a data-integrity issue.

On the core policy question—whether to clamp or reject out-of-range timestamps—René advocates for rejection, calling it "the most honest option." He suggests that the annoyance of rejection might spur users to adopt better solutions (e.g., switching to TAR) or even motivate the creation of a proper ZIP extension for arbitrary dates. His skepticism about `zip(1)`’s clamping behavior ("seems odd") further signals a preference for strict adherence to standards over ad-hoc tool compatibility.

Regarding `git fast-import`, René pushes back against rejecting negative timestamps, arguing that Git’s commit objects already support arbitrarily high or low values as decimal strings. He frames this as a matter of historical record-keeping, citing use cases like importing mainframe files from the 1960s or legal documents from centuries past. While he acknowledges that Git’s in-memory `timestamp_t` is unsigned for historical reasons, he implies this limitation could be revisited if needed, rather than imposing artificial constraints on import.

The thread remains in the discussion phase, with no patches yet. René’s position sharpens the trade-offs: strict correctness (rejection) versus flexibility (clamping or allowing negative timestamps). His comments suggest that any solution will need to justify itself against Git’s broader design principles, not just tool compatibility. The next steps may involve weighing these principles against practical workflows, particularly for `fast-import` users with historical data.
