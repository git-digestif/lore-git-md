This is a substantive policy review of Scott Chacon’s RFC patch that proposes allowing AI-assisted contributions under clear conditions. The patch itself modifies only `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` to replace Git’s current ambiguous stance on AI-generated work with a permissive framework requiring human review, testing, DCO sign-off, and an `Assisted-by:` trailer for substantial AI help.

brian m. carlson’s reply opposes the change on legal and ethical grounds. They argue that current large language models (LLMs) are trained on code from sources that do not permit copying, modification, or distribution, and that LLMs are known to reproduce or closely mimic training-set material. This, they say, makes it impossible for a contributor to honestly assert the Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO) without knowing every source the model used. They also point out that even MIT- or BSD-licensed code requires copyright and permission notices, which AI-generated output typically omits, creating a license violation. Their concern is practical: as a Git distributor, they do not want to risk distributing code they lack the right to distribute, nor do they want to spend limited resources defending against copyright-infringement claims. They cite Gentoo’s and NetBSD’s policies, which take a more restrictive stance, and reference the Contributor Summit discussion where this issue was debated.

The review is substantive: it engages with the legal and ethical implications of the proposed policy, cites specific license requirements, and references external policies and prior project discussions. It does not address the mechanics of the `Assisted-by:` trailer or the wording of the documentation patch itself, but instead challenges the foundational premise that AI-assisted contributions can meet the DCO’s requirements. This is likely to be a point of contention in the thread, as it directly contradicts the author’s claim that responsible AI use can align with existing contributor agreements.
