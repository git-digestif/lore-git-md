This thread is a policy discussion about whether Git should explicitly allow AI-assisted contributions under clear conditions. Scott Chacon proposed amending `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` to permit such contributions, provided the human contributor reviews, understands, tests, and signs off on the submission (DCO), and adds an `Assisted-by:` trailer for substantial AI help. The patch aims to replace Git’s current ambiguous prohibition with a predictable framework, aligning with policies adopted by Linux, Xen, Debian, and the Software Freedom Conservancy (SFC).

The discussion has centered on legal, operational, and philosophical concerns. Scott argues the current policy is unenforceable and that responsible AI use won’t worsen the signal-to-noise ratio, while brian m. carlson opposes the change on legal and ethical grounds, citing risks of training-set material reproduction without proper attribution or license compliance. Junio C Hamano has raised operational concerns about enforceability and reviewer bandwidth, and Luca Milanesio questions the legal framework for authorship and liability. Kristoffer Haugsbakk dismisses the debate as overstated, suggesting the decision should be left to individual contributors.

In the latest reply, brian m. carlson clarifies that the SFC’s guidance—while recommending not to shun contributors who submit AI-generated patches—does not endorse accepting such contributions. They note that several SFC member projects, including QEMU, maintain policies similar to Git’s current prohibition. Brian also highlights the operational burden of low-quality AI-assisted submissions, citing the security list backlog, and the social cost to contributors who reject AI systems but must engage with unwanted patches due to the mailing-list workflow. The debate remains unresolved, with no consensus on whether the proposed text strikes the right balance between openness and enforceability.
