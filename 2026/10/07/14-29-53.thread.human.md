This thread is a policy discussion about whether Git’s `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` should explicitly allow AI-assisted contributions under controlled conditions. Scott Chacon proposed replacing the current ambiguous prohibition with a permissive framework requiring contributors to review, test, and sign off on AI-generated patches (DCO compliance) and add an `Assisted-by:` trailer for substantial AI help. The goal is to align Git with projects like Linux, Xen, and Debian, which have adopted similar policies.

The discussion remains contentious, with no consensus in sight. Scott argues the current policy is unenforceable and that responsible AI use won’t worsen the signal-to-noise ratio, while opponents like brian m. carlson and Luca Milanesio raise legal, ethical, and operational concerns. brian highlights risks of training-set material reproduction without proper attribution, the burden of low-quality AI-assisted bug reports on the security list, and the social cost of forcing contributors to engage with unwanted patches. Luca questions authorship and liability for AI-generated code, and Junio C Hamano worries about enforceability and reviewer bandwidth. Kristoffer Haugsbakk dismisses the debate as overstated, suggesting the decision should be left to individual contributors.

D. Ben Knoble’s recent email adds a philosophical dimension, framing the policy choice as a moral and social decision about Git’s identity. He draws parallels to historical tools and their impact on human work, arguing that AI assistance is not "just another tool" but something that fundamentally shapes the project and its contributors. His cautionary stance aligns with the broader skepticism of brian and Luca, emphasizing the potential long-term harm of premature adoption. The thread remains focused on whether the proposed text strikes the right balance between openness and enforceability, with heightened attention to the operational and social costs of accepting AI-generated patches.
