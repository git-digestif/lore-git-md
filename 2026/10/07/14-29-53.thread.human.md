This thread is a policy discussion about whether Git should explicitly allow AI-assisted contributions under controlled conditions. Scott Chacon has proposed amending `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` to permit such contributions, provided the human contributor reviews, tests, and signs off on the work (DCO), and adds an `Assisted-by:` trailer for substantial AI help. The patch itself hasn’t changed since its introduction, but Scott has responded to Junio C Hamano’s concerns about enforceability and reviewer bandwidth by arguing that the current policy—an outright prohibition—is already unenforceable and that responsible AI use won’t worsen the quality of submissions.

Key participants remain divided. Scott frames the change as a pragmatic update to align Git with peer projects like Linux, Xen, and Debian, and with the Software Freedom Conservancy’s guidance. He cites his own `sha1dc` patch series as an example of how AI-assisted work can be rigorously tested and verified. Junio has raised operational concerns, questioning whether reviewers can realistically assess AI-generated code and whether the policy risks overwhelming the project with low-quality submissions. brian m. carlson and Luca Milanesio oppose the change on legal and ethical grounds, arguing that LLMs may reproduce training-set material without proper attribution or license compliance, and that the legal framework for authorship and liability remains unclear. Kristoffer Haugsbakk dismisses the debate as overstated, suggesting the decision should be left to individual contributors rather than codified in policy.

The thread is now focused on two unresolved questions: whether the proposed requirements are enforceable, and whether Git should adopt a uniform policy or defer to contributors’ risk tolerance. Scott’s latest reply leans toward the SFC’s more detailed disclosure approach but leaves room to align with the Linux kernel’s minimalist "LLM" trailer. No consensus has emerged, and the discussion remains active.
