This is a **performance-focused follow-up** to the `sha1dc-accel` series, introducing a targeted optimization for the x86-64 SHA-NI backend that closes most of the remaining performance gap between the C implementation and the original Rust version. Johannes Schindelin provides a **substantive, tested review** with a patch that avoids reordering the message schedule in the SHA-NI path, reducing the mean `index-pack` runtime on his Ryzen 7 from 8.201s to 8.053s (a 1.8% improvement) and leaving the C implementation only ~1% slower than Rust.

The patch touches five files in the `sha1dc-accel/` subsystem: `internal.h`, `sha1.c`, `ubc_check.c`, `x86.c`, and the unit test file `u-sha1dc.c`. It introduces "mirrored" variants of the SSE2 and AVX2 UBC check functions (`sha1dc_ubc_check_sse2_mirrored`, `sha1dc_ubc_check_avx2_mirrored`) that operate on a reversed message schedule, eliminating the need to reorder the schedule before recompression. The change is technically dense but well-justified by the performance data: the paired mean improvement across 30 randomized `index-pack` runs is 0.147s (95% CI: 0.083–0.211s). Unit tests are extended to verify bit-for-bit compatibility of the mirrored variants.

Schindelin’s review also includes broader validation: he ran the full Git test suite on both a Ryzen 7 (WSL) and a Windows/ARM64 Cloud PC, comparing the original `sha1dc`, `sha1dc-accel`, and the Rust implementation, with zero discrepancies. While his performance measurements are noisy (due to VM environments), they confirm the C and Rust implementations are now effectively equivalent in speed. He explicitly endorses merging the series, with or without his suggested changes, marking a shift from his earlier technical objections to a clear supportive stance.

The thread remains under active review, with no maintainer decision yet. The open question is whether Schindelin’s patch should be folded into the original series or submitted as a follow-up. The technical trade-off is between code complexity (adding mirrored variants and runtime schedule reversal) and the modest but measurable performance gain. The patch is well-tested and addresses a specific, data-backed inefficiency, making it a strong candidate for inclusion.
