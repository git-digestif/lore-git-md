Ben Knoble’s reply challenges the proposal’s premise by clarifying that `git clone` itself doesn’t execute hooks, so the immediate threat model (malicious hooks in a cloned repo) isn’t directly exploitable. Instead, the real risk comes from archives containing a pre-populated `.git/` directory with hooks, which Git *will* execute during later operations. This shifts the focus from "making `clone` safer" to "protecting users from malicious `.git/` directories in archives." Knoble also notes existing mitigations (like `safe.directory` or npm’s `ignore-scripts`) and suggests user education may be the current best defense, though it’s insufficient. The feedback leaves room for refining the proposal or pivoting to address archive-based threats.

Key open questions remain: Should this be a client-side safety measure or involve server-side signaling? How would `--non-intrusive` interact with flags like `--template` or `--config`?
