Thread identity
Subject: [RFC PATCH] SubmittingPatches: allow AI-assisted contributions under clear conditions
Series version: standalone patch (no versioning yet)
Originating author: Scott Chacon <scott@gitbutter.net>
Thread type: policy RFC (documentation change)

Core problem or goal
Amend Git’s `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` to explicitly permit AI-assisted contributions provided the contributor reviews, understands, tests, and signs off on the submission (DCO), and adds an `Assisted-by:` trailer for substantial AI help, briefly describing the tool’s role and the contributor’s verification steps. The goal is to replace the current ambiguous prohibition with a predictable, permissive framework aligned with Linux, Xen, Debian, and the Software Freedom Conservancy’s updated guidance.

Current status
Under review; no prior versions. The patch modifies only `Documentation/SubmittingPatches`, replacing the cautionary paragraph with a new section that lists requirements (review, testing, DCO, `Assisted-by:` trailer). Discussion remains focused on practical, legal, and philosophical viability, with no consensus emerging. Junio C Hamano’s operational concerns (enforceability, reviewer bandwidth, DCO compliance) and brian m. carlson’s legal and ethical objections persist. Scott Chacon’s latest reply frames the current policy as equally unenforceable and argues that responsible AI use will not worsen the signal-to-noise ratio, but brian’s response clarifies that the SFC’s guidance is not an endorsement and that several SFC member projects (including QEMU) maintain policies similar to Git’s current prohibition. The thread is now centered on whether the proposed text strikes the right balance between openness and enforceability, with heightened attention to the operational and social costs of accepting AI-generated patches.

Key technical details
- File modified: `Documentation/SubmittingPatches`
- New trailer: `Assisted-by:` (analogous to `Helped-by:`), required for “substantial” AI assistance
- No code, build system, test, CLI, or on-disk format changes
- No new symbols or renamed functions

Open questions and loose ends
- Whether the `Assisted-by:` trailer should be mandatory for *any* AI assistance or only for “substantial” help (patch currently says “substantial”).
- Whether the trailer’s explanation field should be free-form or follow a prescribed format (e.g., “Tool: X; Verification: Y”); Scott Chacon prefers the SFC’s detailed approach but is open to the Linux kernel’s more minimalist “LLM” trailer.
- Whether reviewers should treat AI-assisted patches differently (e.g., extra scrutiny, additional test coverage).
- Whether the DCO text should explicitly mention AI assistance.
- Legal and ethical feasibility of AI-assisted contributions under the DCO, given potential reproduction of training-set material from non-permissive sources and omission of required copyright/permission notices (brian m. carlson’s concern).
- Enforceability of the proposed requirements—whether reviewers can realistically verify that contributors have reviewed, understood, and tested AI-generated code, and whether the policy risks overwhelming reviewers with low-quality submissions (Junio’s concern).
- Discrepancy with Linux kernel’s guidelines, which avoid naming specific AI tools (to prevent free advertising) and grant maintainers broader discretion to reject contributions outright.
- Whether the rarity of complex code reproduction from training sets justifies the policy change, given the precedent set by Linux and other projects (Scott Chacon’s argument).
- Who bears legal and ethical responsibility for AI-generated contributions—human contributor, LLM, training data, or model provider—and whether the current legal framework can accommodate this ambiguity (Luca Milanesio’s concern).
- Whether AI tools should be restricted to enhancing human capability (e.g., learning, code review, idea generation) rather than generating contributions outright (Milanesio’s interpretation of SFC guidance).
- Whether the project should adopt a uniform policy or defer to individual contributors’ risk tolerance (Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s position).
- Whether the current policy (“do not send in AI-generated slop, period”) is any more enforceable than the proposed permissive framework (Scott Chacon’s counterpoint).
- Operational and social costs of accepting AI-generated patches: reviewer bandwidth, existing backlog on the security list from low-quality AI-assisted bug reports, and the mailing-list workflow’s lack of bot-level filtering (brian m. carlson’s new points).
- Whether the SFC’s guidance is binding or merely advisory, and whether Git should follow the lead of SFC member projects like QEMU that maintain restrictive policies.

Participant positions
- Scott Chacon (author): proposes the change, arguing the current policy creates uncertainty and that responsible AI use should be allowed under the same review standards as human-written patches. Cites SFC’s updated guidance, Red Hat’s DCO interpretation, and parallel policies in Linux, Xen, and Debian. Frames the debate as a choice between Git’s current conservative stance and the more permissive policies adopted by larger projects, positioning the patch as a reasoned middle ground. References Linus Torvalds’ acceptance of AI-assisted contributions under the same DCO and license. Counters Junio’s enforceability concerns by arguing that the current policy is equally unenforceable and that responsible AI use will not worsen the signal-to-noise ratio. Prefers the SFC’s detailed `Assisted-by:` trailer but is open to aligning with the Linux kernel’s more minimalist approach.
- brian m. carlson: opposes the change on legal, ethical, and operational grounds. Argues LLMs may reproduce training-set material from non-permissive sources without proper attribution or license compliance, making it impossible for contributors to honestly assert the DCO. Cites Gentoo’s and NetBSD’s more restrictive policies, the Contributor Summit discussion, and the operational burden of low-quality AI-assisted bug reports on the security list. Clarifies that the SFC’s guidance is not an endorsement and that several SFC member projects (including QEMU) maintain policies similar to Git’s current prohibition. Highlights the social cost of forcing contributors who reject AI systems to engage with unwanted patches due to the mailing-list workflow, contrasting this with projects using pull requests (like Rust) that can block AI-generated content at the bot level.
- Junio C Hamano: raises operational concerns about enforceability, reviewer bandwidth, and DCO compliance. Questions whether the proposed text can be realistically enforced and whether it risks overwhelming reviewers with plausible-sounding but low-quality submissions. Notes the Linux kernel’s approach avoids naming specific tools and gives maintainers broader discretion to reject contributions.
- Luca Milanesio: opposes the change on philosophical and legal grounds. Argues LLMs are fundamentally "copy-and-paste" engines lacking a clear framework for authorship and liability. Questions who would be responsible for malicious code or copyright violations and expresses skepticism about meaningful human review of opaque LLM output. Aligns with SFC’s recommendations but interprets them narrowly: AI tools should enhance human capability (e.g., learning, code review, idea generation) rather than generate contributions outright.
- Kristoffer Haugsbakk: dismisses the policy debate as overstated, arguing the decision to accept legal risks should be left to individual contributors rather than codified in project policy. Rejects the analogy between human and AI-generated contributions as legally and operationally inapposite, and suggests that corporations already regulate knowledge workers’ use of proprietary information via non-compete clauses, implying similar controls could apply to AI-generated code. No substantive engagement with the core legal or ethical concerns raised by other participants.

Related work
- Linux kernel’s `Documentation/process/submitting-patches.rst` (updated in 2024 to allow AI assistance with similar requirements but avoids naming tools and grants maintainers broader rejection discretion).
- Xen Project’s contributor policy (permits AI assistance with disclosure).
- Debian’s AI assistance guidelines (published in 2024).
- Software Freedom Conservancy’s 2025 update to its public guidance on AI and FOSS contributions (recommends not shunning contributors but does not endorse accepting AI-generated patches; several SFC member projects, including QEMU, maintain restrictive policies).
- Gentoo’s and NetBSD’s policies on AI-assisted contributions (more restrictive).
- Contributor Summit discussion on AI and FOSS contributions.
