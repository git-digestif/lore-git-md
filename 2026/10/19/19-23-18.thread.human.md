This thread is about a proposed change to make `git status` more useful in triangular workflows by defaulting to comparing the current branch against both its upstream (`@{upstream}`) and push (`@{push}`) destinations, rather than just the upstream. The patch, now in its third iteration (v3), has sparked a philosophical and cultural debate about Git’s defaults and how they should adapt to users shaped by GitHub’s fork-and-pull model.

Harald Nordgren, the patch’s author, argues that the change benefits less experienced users who may not realize they are missing upstream information when switching to a triangular workflow. The v3 commit message strengthens this justification, emphasizing practical benefits: it reminds users how their branch differs from the last push, signals whether a rebase or rewrite occurred locally (indicating a need for force-pushing), and validates what is about to be pushed. Harald also notes that users in triangular workflows often create their own aliases to check divergence, suggesting the patch reduces friction for those who already rely on such checks. The new default only affects users who configure `push.default` to `current` or `matching`, or set a custom push refspec; otherwise, the output remains unchanged.

Junio C Hamano, the maintainer, remains skeptical. His latest critique focuses on whether `@{push}` divergence is inherently less important to monitor by default than `@{upstream}` divergence. He argues that `@{push}` reflects the user’s own work, making its divergence less critical, and proposes an alternative: external tools (e.g., GitHub) should promote the configuration alongside the `-u` flag rather than changing Git’s defaults. Junio also dismisses the backward-compatibility escape hatch as insufficient justification for flipping the default, framing the debate as a choice between adapting to users’ existing mental models or resisting changes that might reinforce broken understandings of Git’s branching concepts. D. Ben Knoble supports the patch, offering anecdotal evidence from his own workflow where he frequently checks divergence between local and `@{push}` branches to answer questions like *"Have I rebased lately and need to force-push?"*—questions that arise naturally in triangular setups.

The patch itself is technically sound, with minimal changes to `remote.c`, updates to documentation, and comprehensive test coverage for triangular workflows and backward compatibility. However, its readiness for merging hinges on resolving the philosophical debate. No other contributors have weighed in since Junio’s latest reply, leaving the discussion at an impasse. The next steps could involve a stronger argument from Harald or Knoble demonstrating that `@{push}` divergence is frequently surprising or disruptive in practice, a shift in maintainer consensus, or intervention from another contributor (e.g., Phillip Wood) to break the stalemate. Until then, the patch remains under review, with its fate tied to broader questions about Git’s design principles and user experience.
