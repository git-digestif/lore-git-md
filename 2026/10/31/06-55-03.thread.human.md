This thread began with Linus Torvalds submitting a one-line fix for a long-standing bug in `git describe` that miscomputes commit distances when commit timestamps are non-monotonic—an issue that surfaced during the Linux kernel’s v7.4 merge window. The fix prioritizes commit generation numbers (from the commit graph) over unreliable dates, but its effectiveness depends on the commit graph being fresh. Junio Hamano has signaled intent to integrate the patch, and the discussion has since expanded into a broader design conversation about balancing correctness with performance.

The core debate now centers on two complementary proposals to address the fix’s dependency on commit graph freshness. Junio and Derrick Stolee suggest amortizing the performance cost of ensuring generation numbers are valid by writing out a fresh commit-graph after the expensive walk. Linus initially proposed a user-facing `--exact` flag for `git describe` to force accurate results when needed, but Patrick Steinhardt has since argued for flipping the default to `--exact` (opt-out rather than opt-in), accompanied by a warning when the commit graph is unavailable. This reframes the trade-off as a question of user experience: whether intermittent incorrectness (due to timing-dependent graph availability) is more confusing than consistent incorrectness, and whether correctness should be the default behavior.

Key participants are aligned on the technical approach but differ on defaults and user communication. Linus’s patch itself is uncontroversial and likely to land soon, but the thread’s evolution suggests future work on commit-graph write-back mechanisms and user-facing controls. Open questions include whether `--exact` should be the default, how to handle the performance impact on small repositories, and what guardrails should govern state mutation by read-only commands. The Linux kernel’s reliance on `git describe` for versioning adds urgency to resolving these trade-offs.
