This is a policy RFC that revisits the question of whether Git should explicitly allow AI-assisted contributions. The email is Scott Chacon’s reply to brian m. carlson’s opposition, continuing a debate that has already surfaced legal, ethical, and practical concerns.

Scott pushes back on the idea that AI-generated code is uniquely risky. He argues that human contributors also absorb code from many sources—blogs, proprietary projects, news articles—and are not required to account for every influence before signing the DCO. He cites the Software Freedom Conservancy’s 2025 guidance, which Git (as a member project) is encouraged to follow, and points out that the Linux kernel, under the same DCO and license, now permits AI-assisted patches. The comparison is deliberate: Linux’s legal exposure is at least as large as Git’s, yet its policy is more permissive.

The exchange remains sharply divided. brian m. carlson’s concerns—copyright laundering, omitted license notices, and the practical burden on distributors—are not addressed by analogy; they are concrete risks that could trigger legal action. Scott does not dispute the risks, but frames them as manageable under existing review processes and no worse than the risks already accepted for human-written code. He also notes that the Contributor Summit’s “vote” was ambiguous, and that he is offering a concrete draft aligned with the SFC’s recommendations to move the discussion forward.

For a busy reader, the takeaway is that the thread is now a clash between two principled positions: one that sees AI assistance as an extension of normal development practices, and another that sees it as a new vector for legal and reputational harm. Neither side has conceded, and the patch itself is still a straw-man proposal rather than a consensus document. The next replies are likely to focus on whether the DCO’s “right to submit” clause can ever be satisfied for AI-generated material, and whether Git’s smaller scale and volunteer-driven review process can realistically enforce the proposed safeguards.
