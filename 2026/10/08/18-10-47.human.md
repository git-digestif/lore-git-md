This is a philosophical commentary on the ongoing RFC thread about allowing AI-assisted contributions in Git’s `SubmittingPatches` documentation. D. Ben Knoble steps away from the technical and legal debate to share historical and sociological perspectives on tools, their impact on human work, and the broader implications of adopting AI in collaborative projects.

Knoble links to several blog posts (his own, but citing external sources) that explore how tools shape—and are shaped by—their users, arguing that AI is not "just a tool" but something that fundamentally alters the dynamics of creation and responsibility. He references Turkle’s work on human-tool relationships, studies on LLM usage in real-world contexts, and a polemical essay ("AI Pascal’s Wager") that frames rejecting AI contributions as the safer long-term bet for open-source projects. The core of his argument is that tools matter beyond their immediate utility: they influence how we think, work, and interact, and adopting AI without deeper reflection risks unintended consequences.

While Knoble acknowledges Scott Chacon’s proposal includes safeguards (review, testing, DCO compliance), he suggests these may not fully address the philosophical and social dimensions of the debate. His closing remark—preferring the regret of "wish we’d done it sooner" over the risk of irreversible harm—aligns with the cautionary stance of brian m. carlson and Luca Milanesio, though he arrives there via a different route. This email doesn’t engage with the patch’s specifics (e.g., the `Assisted-by:` trailer) but instead invites the thread to consider whether the policy discussion is missing a layer of humanistic critique.

For a reader deciding whether to follow this thread, Knoble’s contribution is a reminder that the debate isn’t just about legal compliance or reviewer bandwidth—it’s also about what kind of project Git wants to be. His links could serve as useful background for those interested in the broader context, but they don’t advance the immediate policy question. The email is unlikely to shift the technical discussion, but it may resonate with participants who see the debate as fundamentally about values, not just mechanics.
