This is a policy discussion reply in the ongoing thread about whether Git should explicitly permit AI-assisted contributions. Luca Milanesio, a contributor, pushes back against Scott Chacon’s proposal to allow AI-generated code under clear conditions, arguing that LLMs are fundamentally different from human learning and experience. He contends that LLMs do not "understand" code but instead statistically assemble fragments, making the human who triggers the generation unqualified to take authorship or responsibility. His concerns center on legal liability (who is accountable for malicious or infringing code?), the practicality of reviewing AI-generated contributions, and the lack of a legal framework for autonomous AI authorship.

Milanesio aligns with the Software Freedom Conservancy’s (SFC) recommendations but interprets them more strictly: AI tools should enhance a developer’s skills, not generate contributions on their behalf. He emphasizes that the human must fully understand and approve every line of code, even if AI assisted in its creation. His position contrasts with Scott’s framing of AI assistance as analogous to human learning, instead likening it to "copy-pasting" without comprehension. The reply does not propose an alternative policy but reinforces the conservative stance, questioning whether Git should follow larger projects like Linux or maintain a more cautious approach given unresolved legal and ethical risks. The tone is skeptical but measured, focusing on practical and legal concerns rather than ideological opposition.
