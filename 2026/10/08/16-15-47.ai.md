This is a policy discussion reply in the ongoing RFC thread about allowing AI-assisted contributions to Git. Brian m. Carlson responds to Scott Chacon’s invocation of the Software Freedom Conservancy’s (SFC) guidance, clarifying that while the SFC recommends not shunning contributors who submit AI-generated patches, it does not endorse accepting such contributions—and in fact, several SFC member projects (including QEMU) maintain policies similar to Git’s current prohibition.

The email centers on three main points. First, brian argues that the SFC’s guidance is not binding and that Git can set its own policy, noting that the SFC also recommends leaving GitHub (which the project has not done). Second, they reiterate the operational concern that accepting AI-generated patches would overwhelm reviewers, citing the existing backlog on the security list from low-quality, AI-assisted bug reports. Third, they highlight a social cost: contributors who reject AI systems would be forced to engage with unwanted patches due to the mailing-list workflow, whereas projects using pull requests (like Rust) can simply block AI-generated content at the bot level.

The tone is measured but firm, positioning the current policy as a pragmatic choice rather than a philosophical one. No new technical details or patch revisions are introduced; the focus remains on legal, operational, and social trade-offs. The reference to QEMU’s policy and the GitHub recommendation underscores that the SFC’s guidance is not monolithic and that other projects have reached different conclusions. This reply is likely to sharpen the debate by framing the issue as a practical, project-specific decision rather than a matter of compliance with external recommendations.
