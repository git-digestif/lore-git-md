This is a policy RFC that updates `Documentation/SubmittingPatches` to explicitly permit AI-assisted contributions under controlled conditions. The patch itself is unchanged from the initial submission, but this reply from Scott Chacon directly addresses Junio C Hamano’s operational and legal concerns with concrete examples and external references.

Chacon acknowledges the enforceability questions Junio raised but argues that the current policy’s ambiguity already fails to prevent low-quality submissions, and that opening the door to responsible AI use won’t materially worsen the situation. He cites his own recent `sha1dc` conversion patch as an example: while he didn’t fully understand every line of the generated code, he reviewed, edited, and rigorously tested it before submission. The patch was motivated by a desire to share a potentially valuable optimization (CPU savings) with downstream users, even though he knew Git’s conservative stance would likely block it. This illustrates the practical tension the policy change aims to resolve: valuable contributions may be withheld due to policy ambiguity, not technical merit.

On the DCO question, Chacon points to a Red Hat legal analysis that interprets the DCO as not requiring every line of a contribution to be the “personal creative expression” of the contributor. He suggests this aligns with the proposed policy’s requirement that contributors review, test, and sign off on AI-generated work. This is a direct rebuttal to Junio’s skepticism about whether contributors can realistically certify the origin and licensing of LLM output.

The exchange also clarifies the divergence between the Linux kernel’s guidelines (which avoid naming specific tools to prevent free advertising) and the Software Freedom Conservancy’s (SFC) recommendations (which ask for tool name, version, and a brief description of the tool’s role). Chacon leans toward the SFC’s more detailed disclosure requirements, given Git’s status as an SFC project, but is open to aligning with the kernel’s approach if that’s preferred.

Junio’s concern about reviewer bandwidth is met with a pragmatic shrug: maintainers already have broad discretion to reject patches, and the proposed text can be adjusted to reflect that. The core of Chacon’s argument remains unchanged: the current policy’s prohibition is ambiguous and overly conservative, and a clear, permissive framework would better serve contributors and reviewers alike. The thread is now at a point where the policy’s viability hinges on whether the project is comfortable with the legal and operational trade-offs Chacon has outlined.
