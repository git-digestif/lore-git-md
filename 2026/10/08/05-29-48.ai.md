This is a policy discussion reply in the ongoing thread about whether Git should explicitly permit AI-assisted contributions. Luca Milanesio, a contributor, pushes back against Scott Chacon’s proposal to allow AI-generated code under clear conditions, arguing that LLMs are fundamentally "copy-and-paste" engines rather than learning systems, and that *fully* AI-generated contributions lack a clear legal or ethical framework for authorship and liability. He questions who would be responsible for malicious code or copyright violations—whether the human "author," the LLM, its training data, or the company behind the model—and expresses skepticism about the feasibility of meaningful review when the human contributor does not fully understand the generated code.

Milanesio aligns with the Software Freedom Conservancy’s (SFC) recommendations but interprets them narrowly: AI tools should be used to *enhance* human capability (e.g., learning, code review, idea generation) rather than to generate contributions outright. He frames his position as a defense of developer growth and accountability, contrasting it with what he sees as the risks of outsourcing authorship to opaque systems. His reply does not engage with the operational concerns Junio C Hamano raised (e.g., reviewer bandwidth) but instead focuses on the philosophical and legal ambiguities of AI-generated contributions, particularly the lack of a clear chain of responsibility. The tone is cautious, emphasizing the need for human oversight and rejecting the idea that LLMs can act as autonomous contributors.
