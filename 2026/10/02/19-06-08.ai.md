This is a substantive critique of the RFC patch series proposing a SHA-256 tree digest signing mechanism for commits and tags. Brian M. Carlson firmly rejects the approach, arguing that it fails to address the core problem of SHA-1's collision resistance weaknesses and that the Git project is already committed to a full transition to SHA-256 as the default object format in Git 3.0.

Carlson's central objection is that the proposed mechanism does not solve the fundamental issue: Git's reliance on SHA-1 for object storage makes it impossible to store colliding blobs, a problem that will only worsen as SHA-1 degrades to MD5-like weakness. He emphasizes that security researchers and other users *need* SHA-256 repositories to store such blobs, and the tree digest approach does nothing to enable this. The critique is grounded in regulatory timelines (the U.S. government will ban SHA-1 by 2030) and ecosystem precedent (TLS, OpenPGP, and major forges have already transitioned or are in the process of doing so).

The email also dismisses the proposal as too late: the Git 3.0 plan has been public for years, discussed at Git Merge 2024, and is scheduled for March 2027. Carlson notes that all major forges already support SHA-256 (at least in preview), while none support the proposed tree digest mechanism, and he anticipates little adoption given the project's prior rejection of "half-transition" designs. He suggests the real solution lies in the interoperability work (rewriting repositories between hash algorithms during clone/fetch), which is not yet upstream but may be contributed by another company.

This is a high-signal reply that shifts the thread's focus from technical design to strategic viability. It frames the RFC as fundamentally misaligned with the project's long-standing SHA-256 migration plan and regulatory realities, rather than a complementary or experimental alternative. The tone is direct but not dismissive; Carlson acknowledges the motivation (ecosystem impact concerns) while arguing that the proposed solution is inadequate.
