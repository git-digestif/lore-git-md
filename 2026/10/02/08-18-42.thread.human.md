This thread began as a technical RFC for a new signing mechanism that embeds SHA-256 digests of tree contents in signed commits and tags, proposed by Scott Chacon as an alternative to the planned Git 3.0 switch to SHA-256 as the default object format. The discussion has since escalated into a strategic debate about whether the Git 3.0 timeline—set for March 2027—is justified or even viable. Chacon’s latest email sharpens this conflict, arguing that the transition effort has been driven almost entirely by GitHub (and its engineers) while lacking broader community buy-in, and that the project’s narrative of ecosystem momentum is misleading.

Key participants remain divided. Chacon continues to advocate for his tree-digest mechanism as a less disruptive solution, framing the Git 3.0 plan as a top-down mandate that risks ecosystem breakage without clear benefits. Brian M. Carlson and Patrick Steinhardt defend the timeline, with Carlson emphasizing regulatory compliance and Steinhardt warning that abandoning the transition would ossify the ecosystem. Chacon’s latest reply directly challenges Steinhardt’s argument, portraying the transition as a GitHub-internal priority rather than a community-wide necessity. Junio Hamano has yet to weigh in on the strategic debate, though he previously raised technical questions about the digest algorithm’s design.

The thread is now at a crossroads. The technical merits of the tree-digest proposal—such as whether it should distinguish file modes or reflect tree structure—remain unresolved, but the larger question is whether the Git project should proceed with the disruptive SHA-256 default switch at all. Chacon’s critique of the transition’s origins and urgency could shift the discussion toward governance and decision-making, particularly if other contributors echo his concerns about corporate influence or the lack of broad engagement. For now, the series remains an RFC with no merge status, but the strategic stakes have grown significantly.
