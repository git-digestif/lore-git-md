The Git mailing list thread on Scott Chacon’s RFC for a tree-digest signing mechanism has evolved into a broader debate about the Git 3.0 plan to switch the default object format to SHA-256 in March 2027. The original proposal—embedding SHA-256 digests of tree contents in signed commits/tags—was framed as a less disruptive alternative to the full SHA-256 transition, but the discussion has since expanded to question whether the Git 3.0 plan itself is strategically sound or even feasible given the current state of tooling and ecosystem readiness.

Key participants remain divided into three camps. Brian M. Carlson and Patrick Steinhardt argue the default switch is non-negotiable, citing ecosystem momentum and regulatory compliance, while Scott Chacon advocates abandoning the Git 3.0 plan in favor of his tree-digest proposal. Johannes Schindelin proposes a middle ground: support SHA-256 without enforcing it as the default, allowing opt-in adoption. Junio C Hamano has yet to weigh in on the strategic debate, though he previously raised technical questions about the digest algorithm’s design. The latest intervention from Kristoffer Haugsbakk, a documentation contributor, shifts the focus to the practical challenges users face. His firsthand account of attempting to migrate a repository to SHA-256 reveals significant gaps in Git’s tooling—`git fast-export`/`fast-import` loses metadata like notes and fails on submodules—and highlights the lack of actionable documentation or a supported migration path. His critique underscores the disconnect between the project’s timeline and the reality for users, who currently have no way to test the transition or provide feedback before the default switch.

The thread now centers on two unresolved questions: whether the Git 3.0 plan should proceed as scheduled, and whether the tree-digest proposal or Schindelin’s opt-in compromise could serve as a viable alternative. Haugsbakk’s experience lends weight to the argument that the transition is premature, while Carlson’s earlier identification of the interoperability work (rewriting repositories between hash algorithms) as the "real solution" remains unaddressed in terms of its availability and completeness. The debate has also exposed tensions around project governance, the role of corporate contributors, and the nature of community consensus. No decisions have been made, and the discussion remains in the design and strategy phase, with the March 2027 deadline looming.
