Thread type: governance proposal (process documentation)

Core problem or goal
Define a structured voting process for the Git project to resolve contentious decisions when traditional consensus-based discussion fails. The immediate motivation is the unresolved AI contributions debate, but the patch is process-only: it specifies *how* to vote, not *what* policy to adopt. The proposal adds a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file, updates `Documentation/DecisionMaking.adoc` to reference it, and wires the new document into the build system.

Current status
Under review; no prior versions. Junio C Hamano (maintainer) has refined the proposal’s framing by rejecting time-bound or proportional eligibility rules as overly complex, reinforcing a binary standard (one person, one vote). The patch remains self-contained with no external dependencies. Junio’s latest feedback narrows the eligibility debate to two options: a simple, inclusive standard (e.g., current low bar) or a stricter but still binary one (e.g., "two or more topics in released versions"). The community must now weigh inclusivity against perceived legitimacy without introducing gradations of influence. Secret ballots remain agreed as a necessary safeguard (Patrick Steinhardt, brian m. carlson, Junio), but logistics (e.g., third-party tools, trusted intermediaries) are unaddressed. Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s ideological critique (that voting inherently favors corporate interests, particularly on AI-related policies) persists, though brian’s reply and the secret ballot consensus may mitigate some concerns.

Open questions and loose ends
- **Voting mechanism**: Still unspecified in the document (flagged as TBD); Junio’s feedback surfaces the need to clarify whether outcomes are determined by plurality (single-option) or proportional support (multi-option).
- **Eligibility criteria**: Junio’s latest reply rules out time-bound filters (e.g., contributions within the last *X* releases) or proportional rules (e.g., weighting by contribution volume), leaving two binary options: a simple standard (current low bar) or a stricter one (e.g., "two or more topics in released versions"). Justin Tobler’s warning about corporate bias in stricter criteria remains unresolved, as does Kristoffer’s critique that *paid* contributors’ votes may align with employer interests. The community must now choose between inclusivity and perceived legitimacy without introducing complexity.
- **Versioning**: Junio suggests using Git OIDs to uniquely identify proposal texts, but implementation details (e.g., how voters reference these OIDs) remain unresolved.
- **Secret ballots**: Patrick Steinhardt, brian m. carlson, and Junio agree that secret ballots are necessary to mitigate concerns about corporate influence and external pressure. However, the logistics of anonymity (e.g., third-party tools, trusted intermediaries) are unaddressed, and Kristoffer’s latest reply does not engage with this proposal, leaving the ideological divide unresolved.
- **Adoption of formal voting**: The central open question—whether the project should adopt any formal voting process at all—remains unanswered. Kristoffer’s critique (that voting inherently favors corporate interests, particularly on AI-related policies) persists, though brian’s reply and the emerging consensus on secret ballots may address some of these concerns. The ideological debate about voting’s neutrality is still unresolved.
- Emily plans to self-review the patch to flag these open questions explicitly.

Participant positions
- **Emily Shaffer (author)**: Proposes the process as a last-resort tool, explicitly framing it as a response to the AI debate stalemate. Suggests using the same approval mechanism (general consensus + `Acked-by:` trailers) that was used for the 2019 Code of Conduct for this document itself.
- **Junio C Hamano (maintainer)**: Constructive and matter-of-fact; accepts the patch’s premise and engages with technical refinements. Rejects time-bound or proportional eligibility rules as overly complex, reinforcing a binary standard (one person, one vote). Prefers a stricter but still binary eligibility criterion (e.g., "two or more topics in released versions") to exclude drive-by contributors but acknowledges the trade-offs raised by Justin Tobler. Implicitly endorses **secret ballots** as a necessary safeguard, aligning with Patrick Steinhardt and brian m. carlson.
- **Kristoffer Haugsbakk (documentation contributor)**: Skeptical of the proposal’s premise, arguing that formal voting would inherently favor corporate interests. His latest reply narrows the critique to *paid* contributors (not all corporate-affiliated contributors), asserting that their votes may in aggregate align with employer interests, particularly on AI-related policies. His critique is ideological, questioning whether voting is the right solution for Git’s governance, and does not engage with the secret ballot proposal or propose alternatives.
- **Patrick Steinhardt (GitLab engineer)**: Pragmatic supporter of the proposal; endorses the *idea* of formal voting but advocates for **secret ballots** to address concerns about corporate influence. His intervention shifts the debate from "whether voting is appropriate" to "how to design voting fairly."
- **Justin Tobler (contributor)**: Aligns with Junio’s concerns about eligibility but cautions against over-correction that could skew representation toward corporate-affiliated contributors. Proposes a time-bound filter (e.g., contributions within the last *X* releases) to exclude dormant contributors while avoiding bias, but Junio’s latest reply rejects this as overly complex. Reinforces Junio’s "topics in released versions" metric over tracking commits in `next`.
- **brian m. carlson (long-time contributor)**: Rebuts Kristoffer’s critique by asserting that corporate-affiliated contributors are capable of independent judgment. Emphasizes personal integrity and endorses **secret ballots** as a safeguard against external pressure (from employers or the community). Their reply shifts the debate toward individual agency and process design, but Kristoffer’s latest reply dismisses this as irrelevant to the structural issue.
