Subject: [PATCH] Documentation: define the voting process
Series version: standalone patch (no series)
Originating author: Emily Shaffer
Thread type: governance proposal (process documentation)

Core problem or goal
Define a structured voting process for the Git project to resolve contentious decisions when traditional consensus-based discussion fails. The immediate motivation is the unresolved AI contributions debate, but the patch is process-only: it specifies *how* to vote, not *what* policy to adopt. The proposal adds a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file, updates `Documentation/DecisionMaking.adoc` to reference it, and wires the new document into the build system.

Current status
Under review; no prior versions. Junio C Hamano (maintainer) has suggested the final document title be "define the voting process" (rather than "propose a voting process") to reflect its authoritative role once merged. The patch is self-contained and does not depend on external work. Junio’s feedback has advanced the proposal by clarifying versioning (using Git OIDs to identify proposal texts), vote-counting mechanics (single-option vs. proportional outcomes), and eligibility criteria (raising the bar to two or more topics in a released version of Git). Patrick Steinhardt has introduced a new safeguard—**secret ballots**—to address concerns about corporate influence on voting outcomes, reframing the debate from "whether voting is appropriate" to "how to design voting fairly." Justin Tobler’s reply surfaces a tension in eligibility design: stricter criteria risk skewing representation toward corporate-affiliated contributors, while looser criteria may admit inactive or drive-by participants. brian m. carlson’s recent reply challenges Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s assumption that corporate-affiliated contributors would inevitably vote in alignment with their employers’ interests, arguing that personal integrity and secret ballots can mitigate such concerns.

Open questions and loose ends
- **Voting mechanism**: Still unspecified in the document (flagged as TBD); Junio’s feedback surfaces the need to clarify whether outcomes are determined by plurality (single-option) or proportional support (multi-option).
- **Eligibility criteria**: Junio proposes a stricter standard (two or more topics in a released version of Git) to exclude drive-by contributors, but Justin Tobler warns this could disproportionately favor corporate-affiliated contributors. Justin also questions whether dormant contributors (e.g., inactive for 15 years) should retain voting rights, suggesting a time-bound filter (e.g., contributions within the last *X* releases). The community must balance recency, impact, and inclusivity to ensure the criteria’s perceived legitimacy. brian’s reply does not directly address eligibility but reinforces the need for secret ballots to protect individual autonomy.
- **Versioning**: Junio suggests using Git OIDs to uniquely identify proposal texts, but implementation details (e.g., how voters reference these OIDs) remain unresolved.
- **Secret ballots**: Patrick Steinhardt and brian m. carlson propose secret ballots to mitigate concerns about corporate influence and external pressure. However, the logistics of anonymity (e.g., third-party tools, trusted intermediaries) are unaddressed. This introduces a new open question about how to reconcile secrecy with the project’s transparency norms, particularly given brian’s argument that anonymity would protect contributors from both employer and community pressure.
- **Adoption of formal voting**: The central open question—whether the project should adopt any formal voting process at all—remains unanswered. Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s critique (that voting inherently favors corporate interests) persists, though brian’s reply and Patrick’s proposal for secret ballots may address some of these concerns. The ideological debate about voting’s neutrality is still unresolved, with brian’s intervention shifting the focus toward individual agency and process safeguards.
- Emily plans to self-review the patch to flag these open questions explicitly.

His intervention shifts the debate from "whether voting is appropriate" to "how to design voting fairly," introducing a new safeguard to mitigate perceived risks. Does not engage with technical details of the patch (e.g., eligibility or versioning) but focuses on governance design.
