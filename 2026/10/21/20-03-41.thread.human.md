This thread is about defining a structured voting process for the Git project to break deadlocks in contentious decisions, such as the ongoing AI contributions debate. Emily Shaffer proposed a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file outlining how votes would work, including eligibility, versioning, and mechanics. The patch is under review, with Junio C Hamano (the maintainer) and Patrick Steinhardt shaping key refinements—Junio clarified versioning (using Git OIDs) and vote-counting, while Patrick introduced the idea of **secret ballots** to address concerns about corporate influence.

The central debate now revolves around **eligibility criteria**. Junio suggested requiring contributors to have authored two or more *topics* in a released version of Git, a stricter standard than the initial proposal. Justin Tobler’s recent email adds nuance to this discussion: he agrees the bar should be higher but warns against over-correction that could skew representation toward corporate-affiliated contributors. He proposes a time-bound filter (e.g., contributions within the last *X* releases) to ensure relevance while avoiding dormant contributors influencing decisions. However, he acknowledges the tension between inclusivity and representativeness, noting that even well-intentioned criteria might not fully capture the project’s diversity.

Other open questions remain unresolved, including the **voting mechanism** (plurality vs. proportional outcomes) and the logistics of **secret ballots** (e.g., trusted intermediaries). Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s earlier critique—that formal voting inherently favors corporate interests—still looms, though Patrick’s secret ballot proposal may mitigate some of these concerns. Emily plans to update the patch to flag these unresolved issues explicitly. The thread is now focused on whether the community can agree on eligibility standards that balance recency, impact, and fairness—critical to the proposal’s legitimacy.
