The Git mailing list is discussing a proposal by Emily Shaffer to define a structured voting process for the project, aimed at resolving contentious decisions like the ongoing AI contributions debate. The patch adds a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file and updates existing governance documentation, with Junio C Hamano (the maintainer) suggesting refinements to clarify the process's authority, versioning (using Git OIDs), and vote-counting mechanics. A key development is the emerging consensus—among Junio, Patrick Steinhardt, and brian m. carlson—that **secret ballots** are necessary to mitigate concerns about corporate influence and external pressure, shifting the debate from *whether* voting is appropriate to *how* to design it fairly.

The main open questions revolve around eligibility criteria and logistics. Junio proposes stricter standards (e.g., contributors with two or more topics in a released version of Git) to exclude drive-by participants, but Justin Tobler warns this could disproportionately favor corporate-affiliated contributors, while Kristoffer Haugsbakk argues that even paid contributors may vote in alignment with employer interests—particularly on AI policy. Kristoffer’s latest reply narrows his critique to *paid* contributors, dismissing brian’s counterargument about individual integrity as irrelevant to systemic bias. Meanwhile, the mechanics of secret ballots (e.g., third-party tools) and whether voting outcomes should be plurality or proportional remain unresolved. The ideological divide persists: some see voting as a pragmatic tool with safeguards, while others view it as inherently flawed for Git’s governance. Emily plans to update the patch to flag these questions explicitly.
