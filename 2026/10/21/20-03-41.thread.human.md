This thread is about defining a structured voting process for the Git project to break deadlocks in contentious decisions, such as the ongoing AI contributions debate. Emily Shaffer proposed a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file outlining how votes would work, including eligibility, versioning, and mechanics, while explicitly avoiding policy specifics like AI rules.

The patch is under review, with Junio C Hamano (the maintainer) engaging constructively on technical refinements like stricter eligibility criteria (requiring contributions to two or more topics in a released version of Git) and using Git OIDs to version proposals. Patrick Steinhardt introduced the idea of **secret ballots** to address concerns about corporate influence, shifting the debate from *whether* to vote to *how* to design voting fairly. Justin Tobler raised concerns that stricter eligibility might skew representation toward corporate-affiliated contributors, while Kristoffer Haugsbakk argued that voting itself would inherently favor corporate interests.

brian m. carlson’s recent reply pushes back against Kristoffer’s critique, asserting that contributors—even those employed by corporations—can vote independently based on personal integrity. They endorse secret ballots as a way to protect voters from external pressure, reinforcing Patrick’s earlier proposal. The thread now centers on balancing transparency, inclusivity, and safeguards against perceived bias, with open questions remaining about eligibility thresholds, vote-counting mechanics, and whether the project should adopt formal voting at all. Emily plans to update the patch to flag these unresolved issues explicitly.
