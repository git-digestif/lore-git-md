This thread is about defining a structured voting process for the Git project to break deadlocks in contentious decisions, such as the ongoing AI contributions debate. Emily Shaffer proposed a new `Documentation/CommunityVoting.adoc` file outlining how votes would work, including eligibility, versioning, and mechanics, while explicitly avoiding policy specifics. The patch is under review, with Junio C Hamano (the maintainer) engaging constructively on refinements like stricter eligibility criteria and using Git OIDs to identify proposals.

The discussion has shifted from *whether* formal voting is appropriate to *how* to design it fairly. A key development is the emerging consensus around **secret ballots** as a safeguard against corporate influence and external pressure, proposed by Patrick Steinhardt and reinforced by brian m. carlson and Junio. This addresses Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s earlier critique that voting would favor corporate interests, though the ideological debate about voting’s neutrality remains unresolved. Justin Tobler has raised concerns that stricter eligibility criteria (e.g., requiring contributions to released versions) could skew representation toward corporate-affiliated contributors, while brian argues that personal integrity and anonymity can mitigate such risks.

Open questions include the logistics of secret ballots (e.g., tooling, trusted intermediaries), whether eligibility should include a time-bound filter to exclude dormant contributors, and the unresolved mechanics of vote counting (plurality vs. proportional outcomes). Emily plans to update the patch to flag these gaps explicitly. The proposal is self-contained and does not depend on external work, but its adoption hinges on resolving these design tensions while balancing inclusivity, fairness, and the project’s transparency norms.
