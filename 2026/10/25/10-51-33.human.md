This is a feature patch that changes the default behavior of `git status` in triangular workflows. It is the third iteration of Harald Nordgren’s proposal to make `git status` compare the current branch against both its upstream (`@{upstream}`) and push (`@{push}`) destinations by default, rather than only the upstream.

The patch itself is small and focused: it modifies `remote.c` to append `@{push}` to the default comparison list, updates the `Documentation/config/status.adoc` man page to reflect the new default, and adds comprehensive tests in `t/t6040-tracking-info.sh` for triangular workflows, common-case scenarios, and branches without an upstream. The backward-compatibility escape hatch remains intact—users can restore the old behavior by setting `status.compareBranches` to `@{upstream}`.

What makes this patch notable is not its technical implementation, which is straightforward and well-tested, but the ongoing philosophical debate it has reignited. The v3 commit message expands significantly on the justification, arguing that even when no one else pushes to the push branch, the comparison is useful for reminding users how their branch differs from what was last pushed, whether it needs a force-push after a rebase, and for validating what is about to be pushed. Harald frames this as a pragmatic adaptation to users’ existing mental models, particularly those shaped by GitHub’s fork-and-pull workflow, where triangular setups are common but often poorly understood.

The debate remains unresolved. Junio C Hamano’s latest critique distinguishes between the *nature* of changes in `@{upstream}` (which may introduce surprises from others) and `@{push}` (which reflects the user’s own work), arguing that the latter is less critical to monitor by default. He has also questioned whether Git should adapt its defaults to accommodate users who may lack a foundational understanding of Git’s branching concepts, suggesting instead that external tools (like GitHub’s UI or documentation) should promote the necessary configuration. D. Ben Knoble’s review counters with a pragmatic, user-centric argument, describing his own workflow where he frequently checks divergence between local and `@{push}` branches to answer questions like *"Have I rebased lately and need to force-push?"*—questions that arise naturally in triangular setups.

The patch is technically ready, but its fate hinges on whether the maintainer and the community can reach consensus on the philosophical and cultural questions it raises. For now, it remains under review, with the justification debate deepening rather than converging. Developers who work in triangular workflows or care about Git’s usability in collaborative environments may want to follow this thread closely.
