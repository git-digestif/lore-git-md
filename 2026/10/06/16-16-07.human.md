This is a strategic reply in the ongoing Git 3.0 SHA-256 transition debate, offering a user-centric perspective on the readiness of Git itself and the broader ecosystem. Kristoffer Haugsbakk, a documentation contributor, shares his firsthand experience attempting to migrate the Git repository to SHA-256 using the officially documented tools (`git fast-export` and `git fast-import`). His account highlights significant usability gaps and fidelity losses in the current migration workflow, reinforcing Scott Chacon’s concerns about the lack of real-world testing and Johannes Schindelin’s critique of unresolved pain points.

Haugsbakk’s attempt to convert the Git repository using `git fast-export --all` reveals several concrete problems: the tool fails on the first error (requiring manual fixes for each subsequent issue), cannot export refs pointing to blobs or trees, and effectively discards Git notes by retaining their SHA-1 filenames. These limitations make the migration process brittle and lossy, even for a one-time conversion. More critically, he notes the absence of a built-in SHA-1-to-SHA-256 mapping mechanism, which would be essential for users who rely on Git notes or need to preserve history during local testing. Brian M. Carlson’s earlier response confirms this gap, leaving users with no practical way to experiment with SHA-256 repositories without risking data loss.

The email’s tone is pragmatic and slightly frustrated, framing the Git 3.0 transition as a top-down mandate with little consideration for non-expert users. Haugsbakk’s hypothetical scenario of a user encountering a "broken" Git 3.0 repository and reverting to SHA-1 with a few commands underscores the risk of widespread opt-out behavior, which could undermine the transition’s goals. His observation that the migration work is being led by a single contributor (Carlson) in their spare time—despite the urgency of government mandates—adds a layer of institutional critique, suggesting the project’s priorities may be misaligned with its stated deadlines.

While Haugsbakk does not engage with the tree-digest proposal directly, his experience validates the concerns raised by Chacon and Schindelin about the transition’s readiness. The email shifts the debate from abstract strategic arguments to concrete usability failures, making it harder to dismiss the Git 3.0 plan as merely a matter of "ecosystem momentum" (as Patrick Steinhardt framed it). His conclusion—that users have no choice but to wait and see what happens in March—highlights the lack of agency afforded to the broader community, a point that may resonate with contributors who feel the transition is being driven by a small group of insiders.
