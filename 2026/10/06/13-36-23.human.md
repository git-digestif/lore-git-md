This is a strategic reply in the ongoing debate about Git 3.0’s SHA-256 transition and Scott Chacon’s tree-digest signing proposal. Johannes Schindelin (Dscho) weighs in with a nuanced perspective that bridges the positions of Chacon and Patrick Steinhardt, while raising practical concerns about the SHA-256 migration’s feasibility and real-world impact.

Schindelin acknowledges the ecosystem’s reluctance to adopt SHA-256, attributing it to both a lack of urgency (SHA-1 collisions remain expensive and impractical for Git objects) and the daunting technical challenges of migration. He highlights unresolved issues—submodules, shallow clones, and the ambiguity of short OIDs—that make a full transition impractical for many users. His analysis suggests the cryptographic rationale for switching to SHA-256 is often overstated: most projects rely on external signatures (e.g., package managers) rather than Git’s hashes for security, and the real-world risk of SHA-1 collisions is dwarfed by social engineering attacks.

While Schindelin agrees that the Git 3.0 deadline has spurred progress, he argues the project should remain flexible. He proposes that Git 3.0 could ship with *optional* SHA-256 support—well-tested and robust—but not mandate it as the default. This would avoid forcing users into a disruptive transition while still enabling those who need SHA-256 to adopt it. His framing aligns with Chacon’s critique of the transition’s top-down urgency but stops short of endorsing the tree-digest proposal as a full alternative. Instead, he suggests the default switch should only happen if users *demand* it to solve a recognized problem.

The email adds weight to Chacon’s argument that the SHA-256 transition lacks broad buy-in, while avoiding the confrontational tone of earlier replies. Schindelin’s technical objections (e.g., submodule handling, short OID ambiguity) are likely to resonate with developers who’ve grappled with migration challenges. His call for flexibility could shift the debate toward a compromise: delaying the default switch while ensuring SHA-256 support is production-ready for those who need it.
