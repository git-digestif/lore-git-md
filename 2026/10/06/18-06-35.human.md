This is the sixth installment in Taylor Blau’s seven-part summary of the Git Contributor’s Summit 2026, covering the discussion on **AI contribution policy**. The thread is a high-level design and governance conversation, not a code patch, and remains in the discussion phase with no decisions or merges yet.

The session explored whether and how Git should allow AI-generated contributions, balancing practical benefits (proofreading, language cleanup, idea generation) against legal, ethical, and quality concerns. The current policy, as stated in `Documentation/SubmittingPatches`, is ambiguous about whether contributors can certify AI-generated output under the Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO). Participants noted that AI-assisted patches often have a distinct, sometimes awkward style, and that attribution (e.g., an `Assisted-by:` trailer) could help reviewers assess quality and trustworthiness.

Key tensions emerged: some saw AI as a useful tool for improving writing and generating ideas, while others worried about legal risks, loss of trust, and an influx of low-quality contributions. Brian m. Carlson and Patrick Steinhardt voiced strong reservations, with Brian emphasizing the implicit use of others’ code in AI-generated output and Patrick warning about the potential for overwhelming the project with poor-quality patches. Peff (Jeff King) and Emily Shaffer advocated for a more permissive approach, noting that other open-source projects (like the Linux kernel) are more lenient and that Git risks missing out on useful tools by being overly conservative.

The group agreed to formalize the policy through a structured process: proposals will be drafted, reviewed by the Software Freedom Conservancy (SFC) legal team, and then put to a vote among active contributors (likely those with at least 50 merged patches). Emily Shaffer volunteered to lead the voting procedure, drawing on her experience with Jujutsu. The outcome could range from maintaining the status quo to explicitly allowing AI-assisted contributions with clear attribution requirements. Until then, the project’s stance remains cautious and undefined, leaving contributors and reviewers in a gray area.
