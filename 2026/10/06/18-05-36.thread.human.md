The Git Contributor's Summit 2026 thread continues to refine its most contentious topic: the community voting process for resolving divisive decisions like the AI contribution policy. All seven original discussion topics from the summit have been delivered, but the governance debate now dominates, with the voting eligibility criteria under active, substantive review.

The latest proposal comes from Phillip Wood, who suggests a hybrid eligibility model: require a minimum number of commits *over all time* (to ensure a track record) while also mandating *at least one recent commit* (to ensure ongoing engagement). This approach avoids rigid time windows like "N commits in the last M years" while still filtering out inactive contributors. It directly engages with Jeff King's (Peff) empirical data on commit-count thresholds, noting that under low thresholds (e.g., 5–10 commits), most long-term contributors would qualify within the last two years anyway. However, if the threshold is raised (e.g., to 50 or 200 commits), the hybrid model becomes more meaningful, balancing inclusivity with practicality. This proposal addresses concerns about "drive-by" contributions raised by brian m. carlson and Kristoffer Haugsbakk, while avoiding the pitfalls of strict time-based cutoffs that Junio C Hamano opposes.

The thread remains in discussion phase, with no decisions or merges reported. The hybrid model joins other proposals on the table, including Peff's data on commit-count distributions, Tuomas Ahola's suggestion to use the average commit count (26) as a threshold, and Patrick Steinhardt's call for a middle ground (25–30 commits). Key unresolved questions include whether to count individual commits or patch series, how to define "recent" activity, and whether to include non-code contributions like bug reports. The voting mechanism itself (e.g., Debian's *devotee*, email-based systems) and tooling neutrality remain open, with brian m. carlson opposing GitHub-based voting. The AI contribution policy vote now hinges on resolving these governance details, with Emily Shaffer volunteering to coordinate logistics once the process is finalized.

Other topics from the summit remain actionable but less active. The security process documentation is being drafted by Peff, Git 3.0 planning has consensus on a two-stage rollout and SHA-256 support, and documentation tooling is poised to standardize on Asciidoctor after Fedora compatibility concerns were resolved. Outreachy sponsorship outreach continues, while the pluggable ODB and Protocol v2 for pushes topics await further technical development. The community voting process, however, is the critical path for resolving the AI policy stalemate and establishing a template for future governance decisions.
