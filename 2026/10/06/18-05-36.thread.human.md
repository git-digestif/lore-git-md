The Git Contributor's Summit 2026 thread continues to refine its most contentious topic: the community voting process for resolving divisive decisions like the AI contribution policy. The latest discussion, led by Jeff King (Peff), proposes a hybrid eligibility model to balance inclusivity with practicality. Peff suggests an algorithmic threshold—such as a minimum number of commits combined with recent activity—to automatically qualify most contributors, while maintaining a human-curated list for edge cases like former maintainers (e.g., Linus Torvalds) or deeply involved but inactive contributors (e.g., Emily Shaffer). This approach aims to address Emily’s concern about excluding long-term contributors while keeping administrative overhead manageable, with Junio C Hamano or the Project Leadership Committee (PLC) overseeing the exceptions list.

The thread has narrowed to four key open questions: the voting platform (CIVS vs. devotee), the voter eligibility formula (commit count, topic count, time-limited activity, or non-code contributions), how to operationalize qualitative contributions (e.g., reviews, documentation, community support), and the technical requirement to reference proposals by Message-ID rather than commit ID. Peff’s hybrid model offers a potential resolution to the eligibility debate, but specifics—such as the exact threshold (e.g., 25–30 commits with a 2–3 year window, as Patrick Steinhardt suggested) and the governance of the exceptions list—remain unresolved. Junio’s latest email broadened the scope to include non-code contributions, adding complexity to the measurement challenge. Meanwhile, the AI contribution policy vote, which hinges on this process, remains on hold until these questions are settled. Emily Shaffer plans to reroll the patch after November 12, incorporating consensus on the platform, eligibility rules, and operational details.
