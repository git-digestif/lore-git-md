The Git Contributor's Summit 2026 thread has grown into a sprawling discussion covering seven major topics and now fifteen follow-ups on governance, with the community voting process emerging as the most active and contentious item. The latest exchange between Junio Hamano and Emily Shaffer shifts the focus from rigid commit-count thresholds to a broader, more inclusive definition of what constitutes a meaningful contribution to the project. This reframing responds to Emily’s concern about being excluded from voting due to her reduced capacity for code contributions in recent years, but it also complicates the practical design of the voting system.

The core of the debate now centers on how to balance inclusivity with practicality. Junio argues that documentation, reviews, and even informal support (e.g., mentorship or community engagement) should count toward voter eligibility, aligning with Emily’s call for qualitative inclusivity. However, this raises questions about how to measure such contributions fairly and transparently—whether through self-reporting, tracking mailing-list or IRC activity, or expanding the criteria to include substantive reviews (as Phillip Wood proposed earlier). The unresolved tension here is between a system that is scriptable and objective (e.g., counting commits or patch series) and one that acknowledges the full spectrum of contributions to Git’s ecosystem.

The thread remains in the design phase, with no decisions or merges yet. The two key open questions—voting platform (CIVS vs. devotee) and voter eligibility—now have a third dimension: how to operationalize Junio’s call for inclusivity without sacrificing the system’s integrity. Phillip Wood’s hybrid model (requiring both a track record and recent activity) and Tuomas Ahola’s data-driven proposal (using the average commit count of 26) offer potential middle grounds, but Junio’s latest email suggests the project may need to go further, potentially incorporating non-code contributions into the eligibility criteria. This could delay the finalization of the voting process, which is now critical for resolving other contentious topics like the AI contribution policy.

Other topics from the summit remain actionable but are progressing more smoothly. The security mailing list process is moving forward with Peff drafting a public proposal, and Git 3.0 planning has consensus on a two-stage rollout (2.99 → 3.0) with SHA-256 support and Rust inclusion. Documentation tooling is now unblocked after Junio confirmed pre-built documentation has used Asciidoctor since mid-2024, removing the last infrastructure hurdle. Outreachy sponsorship and pluggable ODB integration are also advancing, though funding and technical gaps remain. The Protocol v2 for pushes topic is poised for a patch series, with no objections in principle to the proposed optimizations. For now, the community voting process is the bottleneck, and Junio’s latest intervention ensures it will remain a lively discussion for weeks to come.
