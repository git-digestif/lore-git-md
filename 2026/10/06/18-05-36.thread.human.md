The Git Contributor's Summit 2026 thread has grown into a sprawling discussion covering seven major topics and now nineteen follow-ups focused on governance—specifically, the design of a formal community voting process to resolve contentious decisions like the AI contribution policy. The thread is now in its refinement phase, with all original follow-ups delivered and the community voting process patch under active, substantive review.

The core of the current discussion revolves around five open questions about the voting process, with recent emails narrowing the debate to the governance of a human-curated list for edge cases. Jeff King (Peff) proposed a hybrid eligibility model combining an algorithmic threshold (e.g., "X commits in the last Y years") with a human-curated list for former maintainers or deeply involved but inactive contributors. Patrick Steinhardt’s latest email advances this by questioning whether long-inactive contributors should even qualify and insisting on full transparency for the human-curated list—public documentation of who added each contributor, the reasoning behind their inclusion, and advance notice to allow for challenges. This addresses concerns about bias or arbitrary decisions but leaves unresolved whether the list should be opt-in or opt-out and who should maintain it (Junio, the PLC, or another body).

The eligibility threshold itself remains contentious. Earlier proposals ranged from Junio’s flat five-commit threshold to Peff’s empirical data showing 42 contributors would qualify under a 50-commit, two-year window. Tuomas Ahola suggested using the average commit count (26) as a middle ground, while Phillip Wood proposed a hybrid model requiring a minimum number of commits *over all time* plus at least one recent commit. Jeff King’s hybrid model now combines an algorithmic threshold with the human-curated list, but the project must still decide how to operationalize the algorithmic criteria (e.g., commit count, time window, patch series vs. individual commits) and whether to include non-code contributions (e.g., documentation, reviews) in the algorithmic threshold or the human-curated list.

On the voting platform, Jonathan Corbet’s review of CIVS confirmed its practicality for occasional polls but highlighted a usability hurdle: voters must proactively sign up on the platform, which could create friction for Git’s email-centric workflow. The project must now weigh CIVS’s simplicity against devotee’s self-hosted neutrality, with no clear consensus yet.

Other topics remain actionable but unresolved. The security mailing list process is being documented by Peff, Git 3.0 planning has consensus on a two-stage rollout (2.99 → 3.0) and SHA-256 support but unresolved questions about LTS status and Rust mandates, and the documentation tooling debate is now unblocked (Fedora compatibility resolved) but lacks funding or structural decisions. The Outreachy sponsorship topic awaits follow-up outreach, the pluggable ODB effort has a repository extension planned for Git 2.57 but known gaps in commit-graph and MIDX support, and the AI contribution policy vote now hinges on the community voting process’s adoption.

Key participants include Junio (advocating for broader inclusivity in eligibility), Peff (proposing the hybrid model and empirical data), Phillip Wood (refining eligibility criteria and hybrid models), Emily Shaffer (coordinating the AI policy vote and patch), and Patrick Steinhardt (pushing for transparency in the human-curated list). The thread remains in discussion phase with no decisions or merges reported, but the recent focus on the human-curated list’s governance brings the project closer to a workable voting process.
