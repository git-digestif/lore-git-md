This is a strategic reply in the ongoing Git 3.0 SHA-256 transition debate, focusing on the practical realities of repository migration and the human factors behind the interoperability work. Brian M. Carlson responds to Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s critique of Git’s tooling gaps by providing a concrete, if imperfect, workaround for converting repositories to SHA-256 using `git fast-export` and `git fast-import`. His message is equal parts technical demonstration and candid reflection on the challenges of open-source maintenance, offering a rare glimpse into the personal toll of leading a high-stakes, under-resourced effort.

Carlson shares a shell script that successfully converts the Git repository (including its problematic SHA-1 collision submodule) to SHA-256 by processing submodules separately and using mark files to rewrite references. The script is functional but admittedly slow and cumbersome—far from the polished, user-friendly tooling Haugsbakk sought. Its existence since Git 2.30 proves that migration is *possible*, but the poor user experience underscores why few have attempted it. Carlson’s example is less a solution than a proof of concept, demonstrating that the interoperability work is further along than Haugsbakk assumed but still far from production-ready.

The email’s most revealing section addresses the *why* behind the tooling gaps. Carlson explains that the interoperability work (which would enable seamless repository rewriting during clone/fetch) hasn’t been upstreamed because it depends on Patrick Steinhardt’s pluggable ODB effort—a refactoring so disruptive that it requires constant rebase work. His admission of burnout ("I'm presently very burnt out, so I'm doing very little coding in my free time") humanizes the institutional critique Haugsbakk raised. The SHA-256 transition, mandated by government deadlines and corporate needs, is being driven by a single volunteer working in their spare time, while the broader community prioritizes performance and scalability. Carlson’s comparison to other one-maintainer projects (bash, ncurses) frames this as a systemic issue in open source, not a Git-specific failure.

On the technical front, Carlson clarifies that the interoperability work doesn’t handle notes because it’s designed for clone/fetch operations, where notes aren’t typically copied. He expresses interest in adding in-place rewriting (which would preserve notes) but offers no timeline. This leaves Haugsbakk’s core concern—users losing metadata during migration—unresolved, though Carlson’s tone suggests it’s a solvable problem given sufficient resources.

The email doesn’t engage with the broader strategic debate (e.g., Scott Chacon’s tree-digest proposal or Johannes Schindelin’s opt-in compromise) but implicitly reinforces the urgency of the Git 3.0 timeline. Carlson’s workaround, while functional, is a stopgap; the real solution—the interoperability work—remains stalled by technical debt and maintainer bandwidth. His message is a plea for patience and a call to action: the project’s SHA-256 goals are achievable, but not without sustained effort from contributors beyond himself. For a busy reader, the takeaway is clear: the Git 3.0 transition is technically viable but operationally fragile, and its success hinges on addressing both the code *and* the human factors behind it.
