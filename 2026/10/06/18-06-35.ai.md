This is the sixth installment in Taylor Blau’s seven-part series summarizing the Git Contributor’s Summit 2026. It covers the discussion on **AI contribution policy**, a topic that has been simmering on the list for months but had not previously been addressed at a summit. The email is a lightly edited transcript of the round-table conversation, not a patch or proposal, so there is no code, no merge status, and no immediate action items. What it does is surface the project’s current uncertainty and sketch a path toward resolution.

The core tension is familiar: Git’s current policy (as documented in `SubmittingPatches`) is silent on whether AI-generated code can satisfy the Developer Certificate of Origin (DCO). The DCO requires contributors to certify they have the right to submit the work under Git’s license; AI tools trained on public repositories complicate that certification. Participants agree the status quo is too conservative—it effectively bans meaningful use of AI tools—but they disagree on how permissive the new policy should be. Some (notably brian m. carlson) worry about legal risk and erosion of trust; others (Peff, Emily Shaffer) see the policy as unnecessarily restrictive and want to learn from other open-source projects.

The discussion converges on a two-phase process. First, key participants (brian, Taylor, and possibly others) will draft competing policy proposals and submit them to the Software Freedom Conservancy (SFC) for legal review. Second, the project will hold a vote among active contributors (defined loosely as those with at least 50 merged patches) to choose among the proposals. Emily Shaffer volunteers to coordinate the voting logistics, drawing on her experience with Jujutsu’s governance. The thread leaves open whether the vote will be binding or merely advisory; Peff notes that Junio Hamano, as maintainer, has final authority but typically follows the consensus.

Key technical details for future reference: the `Assisted-by` trailer (introduced by Johannes Schindelin) is mentioned as a model for attribution; the Linux kernel’s more permissive stance is cited as a point of comparison; and the Debian project’s proposal-and-vote process is floated as a procedural template. No timeline is set, but the next steps are clear: draft proposals, legal review, and then a vote. Until then, the project remains in limbo—AI-assisted contributions are neither explicitly forbidden nor explicitly allowed, and contributors are left to guess what will be acceptable.
