This is a strategic reply in the ongoing Git 3.0 SHA-256 transition debate, focusing on the practical state of the interoperability work—the "real solution" to SHA-1 deprecation that Brian M. Carlson previously identified. Carlson responds to Kristoffer Haugsbakk’s critique of Git’s migration tooling by providing a concrete, if imperfect, workaround for converting repositories to SHA-256, while also acknowledging the limitations of the current approach and the personal burnout that has slowed progress on the upstream interoperability code.

The email centers on a shell script (`convert-git`) that demonstrates how to rewrite a repository (including submodules) from SHA-1 to SHA-256 using `git fast-export` and `git fast-import`. The script handles the Git repository and its `sha1collisiondetection` submodule in sequence, using mark files to map object IDs between hash algorithms. While functional, Carlson emphasizes its downsides: it is slow, produces different results than the true interoperability code, and offers a poor user experience. He notes that this approach has been available since Git 2.30, making it a viable (if cumbersome) option for users who want to experiment with SHA-256 today. The script could be generalized to work on any repository with minimal effort, addressing one of Haugsbakk’s key concerns about the lack of accessible tooling.

Carlson also clarifies why the interoperability work has not progressed further upstream: the pluggable ODB effort (a prerequisite for the interoperability code) has introduced widespread breakage, requiring extensive rebasing and fixes. His personal burnout—exacerbated by the demands of maintaining this complex, low-priority work in his spare time—has further slowed progress. He expresses a desire to implement in-place rewriting (which would preserve Git notes) but frames it as a future goal rather than an immediate solution. This context underscores the institutional challenges Haugsbakk highlighted: the SHA-256 transition is being driven by a single contributor working outside their paid role, despite its regulatory and ecosystem-wide implications.

The reply also touches on the broader open-source sustainability problem, comparing Git’s situation to other critical projects maintained by individuals (e.g., bash, ncurses). Carlson’s tone is resigned but pragmatic, acknowledging the mismatch between the project’s needs and the resources available. He reiterates that while companies prioritize performance and scalability, foundational work like hash-function transitions receives little attention—a dynamic that has left the interoperability effort in limbo.

For future summarizers, the key details are:
- **Tooling status**: The `convert-git` script provides a functional (if slow and imperfect) way to rewrite repositories to SHA-256, including submodules. It is not user-friendly but could be generalized for broader use.
- **Interoperability work blockers**: The pluggable ODB effort has introduced widespread breakage, requiring significant rebasing and fixes before the interoperability code can be upstreamed. Carlson’s burnout has further delayed progress.
- **Notes preservation**: The interoperability work does not currently rewrite notes, but Carlson suggests this could be added to in-place rewriting in the future.
- **Strategic implications**: The email reinforces Haugsbakk’s critique of Git’s tooling gaps while offering a partial workaround. It also highlights the institutional challenges of driving a major transition with limited resources, lending weight to Schindelin’s call for opt-in adoption and Chacon’s argument that the Git 3.0 plan is premature.
- **Participant positions**: Carlson remains committed to the Git 3.0 timeline but acknowledges the practical difficulties of the transition. His response does not engage with the tree-digest proposal or Schindelin’s compromise, focusing instead on the logistics of the interoperability work. The tone is pragmatic rather than confrontational, emphasizing the constraints of open-source development.
