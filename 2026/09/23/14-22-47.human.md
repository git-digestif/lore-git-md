This is a follow-up in the ongoing discussion about Git's security mailing list and release process, sparked by Johannes Schindelin's summit recap. Toon Claes, a GitLab contributor, responds to Schindelin's observations with practical proposals and personal commitments.

Toon agrees with the summit consensus that the security list is overwhelmed by AI-generated or duplicate reports, and that waiting for an empty queue is not a viable release criterion. He plans to drive the effort to document Git's security model, a task Schindelin noted was missing, and will revisit an old discussion about Git's security boundary started by Derrick Stolee. On the topic of moving non-security bugs to the public list, Toon acknowledges the staffing shortage but does not propose a solution beyond GitLab's commitment to invest more resources in triage.

A key point of disagreement is Schindelin's suggestion of a timeout for public discussion of security issues. Toon opposes this, arguing that other priorities (like documenting the process) should come first. He also clarifies that the confidentiality risk of using AI for triage—not copyright—is the primary concern, echoing the summit's mixed views on AI tools.

The most actionable part of the email is Toon's offer to collaborate closely with Schindelin on the next security release. He admits to lacking knowledge of the release process (merging, backporting, advisory preparation) and asks to shadow Schindelin to learn. This addresses Schindelin's observation that process knowledge is siloed and could help distribute the workload. Toon suggests adopting Git for Windows' three-week release cadence for routine fixes but leaves open whether urgent issues (like Elijah Newren's 0-day) should follow a faster timeline.

The tone is collaborative and solution-oriented, focusing on concrete next steps rather than rehashing problems. For readers tracking Git's security process, this email signals a likely near-term release and a shift toward better documentation and knowledge sharing. The offer to shadow Schindelin is particularly notable—it could reduce the bus factor for security releases if successful.
