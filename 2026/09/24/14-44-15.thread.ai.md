Thread identity: Documentation patch series introducing `gitmergeconflicts(7)`, a new man page consolidating merge conflict resolution guidance. Series version: v1 (initial, pending mechanical integration fixes, `command-list.txt` registration, content refinements including cherry-pick-specific adjustments, foundational explanations, terminology clarifications, new tooling advice, and Patrick Steinhardt’s proposed commit-accessibility enhancement). Originating author: Julia Evans (via GitGitGadget). Thread type: patch series.

Core problem or goal: Git’s current documentation scatters merge-conflict advice across multiple command man pages (`git merge`, `git rebase`, `git revert`, `git cherry-pick`, `git pull`), leading to duplication, omissions, and user confusion. The series introduces a dedicated `gitmergeconflicts(7)` guide to centralize this information, improve clarity (e.g., clarifying "ours" vs. "theirs"), and provide practical examples based on user feedback. The goal is to replace redundant sections in existing man pages with cross-references to the new guide, reducing maintenance burden and improving user experience. The series now explicitly balances centralization with preserving command-specific nuances, particularly for operations like `git cherry-pick` that involve unique pseudo-refs (`CHERRY_PICK_HEAD`) and workflows. Junio’s and Patrick Steinhardt’s reviews have clarified that the new guide is not a complete substitute for removed sections, especially for cherry-pick-specific details, which must be retained in the command’s man page. The guide’s terminology, foundational explanations, and tooling advice are also under refinement to improve clarity and practical utility, with Julia Evans prioritizing user-tested practicality over theoretical completeness. Recent discussion has elevated two concrete improvements: (1) making commit information (messages or IDs) more accessible during conflict resolution, and (2) promoting `merge.conflictstyle=diff3` as a best practice, with Junio C Hamano and Patrick Steinhardt strongly advocating for its default adoption. Patrick Steinhardt’s latest proposal introduces a **commit-accessibility enhancement** that embeds commit details (IDs, messages, dates) directly into conflict markers, transforming them from passive indicators into active resolution tools. Julia Evans has enthusiastically endorsed this proposal, highlighting its practical benefits (e.g., commit messages and dates providing immediate context) and suggesting it may be actively promoted in the guide, though the exact implementation remains under discussion. This enhancement is a significant user-experience improvement and may evolve into a follow-up patch after prototyping.

The fifth patch (updating `git-cherry-pick.adoc`) has drawn substantive feedback from Junio, who argues that critical cherry-pick-specific details—such as the `CHERRY_PICK_HEAD` pseudo-ref and the behavior of cleanly auto-resolved paths—were removed without being adequately covered in the new guide. Junio’s latest clarification emphasizes that `CHERRY_PICK_HEAD` is available *any* time a cherry-pick stops due to conflict, regardless of whether one or multiple commits are being picked, and serves the same role for cherry-picks as `MERGE_HEAD` does for merges. This reinforces the need to retain these details in the cherry-pick man page rather than moving them to the new guide. Julia has committed to revising the patch to retain these details while still linking to the new guide for general conflict-resolution advice. Jeff King (Peff) provided workflow guidance, advising against routinely applying patches to `seen` before submission to catch integration issues, as it more often reveals conflicts the maintainer would handle anyway. Instead, he suggests contributors target Junio’s `next` or `jch` branches for early integration testing, noting these are more stable and representative of topics likely to graduate. Junio expanded on this guidance, clarifying that the recommended approach is to merge a topic branch *into* an integration branch (e.g., `next` or `jch`) to uncover interactions with other in-flight work, rather than applying patches directly onto it. This can reveal textual merge conflicts, API changes, or even useful helper functions introduced by other topics. Junio noted that this approach is especially valuable for contributors working in areas with overlapping topics but may yield little for isolated documentation work like Julia’s. He also reinforced that topics must be in his `jch` branch before he marks them for `next`, making `jch` a reliable target for early integration testing.

Patrick Steinhardt’s recent review of the first patch adds further refinements, focusing on **terminology and jargon**, **technical completeness**, and **user experience**. He questions the intuitiveness of terms like "area" (instead of "hunk") and "ours/theirs," and proposes rewording the "ours/theirs" explanation to clarify that `HEAD` always determines "ours" during a merge, regardless of the command (`git merge` vs. `git rebase`). This aligns with Git’s internal logic and avoids potential confusion. He also suggests expanding the introduction to briefly explain how Git performs a 3-way merge, including the role of the common ancestor (base), to provide foundational context for users. Additionally, he proposes making the commits involved in a conflict more accessible (e.g., via `git rev-parse --reference` commands) and mentions `git merge-tool --tool-help` as a way to list available merge tools. Julia Evans has responded to these suggestions, accepting some (e.g., adding `linkgit:` macros, mentioning `git merge-tool --tool-help`, correcting minor typos) while pushing back on others. She prefers the term "lines of code" over "hunk" or "area" for accessibility, and opts for a concise "ours/theirs" explanation that acknowledges the confusion rather than attempting to resolve it. She also declines to include a 3-way merge explanation in the initial version, citing user feedback that found such explanations confusing, though she leaves the door open to adding it later as an optional deep dive. The discussion about commit accessibility (e.g., `git rev-parse --reference`) remains unresolved, with Julia expressing skepticism about the value of commit IDs but openness to including commit messages.

Patrick Steinhardt’s latest email introduces two **substantive improvements** to the new guide: (1) **commit accessibility** and (2) **`merge.conflictstyle=diff3` as a best practice**. For commit accessibility, Patrick proposes embedding commit details (IDs, messages, dates) directly into conflict markers, e.g.:
```
<<<<<<< HEAD: abcdefg (fruits: add apple, 2026-10-01)
    "cherry",
=======
    "banana",
>>>>>>> add-fruit: 12345678 (fruits: add banana, 2024-02-03)
```
This approach avoids requiring users to learn special refs like `MERGE_HEAD`, provides context via commit messages and dates, and aligns with Git’s existing conflict-marker syntax. Julia Evans has enthusiastically endorsed this proposal, highlighting the practical benefits of including commit messages and dates, which provide immediate context about the intent and timing of conflicting changes. While she stops short of fully embracing Patrick’s vision of embedding commit IDs directly into markers, she suggests making commit information *discoverable* via commands like `git show HEAD` or `git show add-fruit`. The enhancement is a significant user-experience improvement and may evolve into a follow-up patch after Patrick’s planned GitLab prototype. For `diff3`, both Patrick and Junio strongly endorse making it the default conflict style, citing its clarity and user feedback. Julia Evans’ observation that "every time I show people diff3 someone tells me how happy they are" reinforces its practical impact. The guide is now expected to actively promote `diff3` as a best practice, and this discussion may foreshadow a broader shift in Git’s default behavior.

The second patch’s removal of introductory context in `git-merge.adoc` has been flagged as potentially abrupt for users who encounter it without prior knowledge of merge conflicts. While the patch itself is mechanically sound—replacing duplicated content with a cross-reference—Patrick notes that the `git-merge` man page may now feel abrupt to users who encounter it without prior knowledge of merge conflicts. This feedback aligns with earlier substantive reviews advocating for foundational explanations in the new guide. Julia Evans has now addressed this gap by proposing a new "WHAT IS A MERGE CONFLICT?" section for the `gitmergeconflicts(7)` guide, adopting a lightweight, example-driven approach that avoids technical jargon like "3-way merge" or "common ancestor." The section clarifies that conflicts arise when overlapping changes cannot be automatically combined, and describes Git’s behavior during conflicts (staging successfully merged files, leaving conflicted files unstaged with markers). This addition directly responds to D. Ben Knoble’s feedback and is likely to be included in the next revision (v2), alongside the previously agreed `git log --merge -p` guidance.

This aligns the documentation with the current state of the feature.
