This is a follow-up in the Git version numbering thread, specifically a response from Junio C Hamano to Phillip Wood’s proposal for a `core.defaults` meta-config variable and a consequentialist approach to default changes. Junio engages with both ideas but raises practical concerns about their feasibility and effectiveness.

Junio acknowledges that the project already adopts a consequentialist mindset for some changes (e.g., the default merge strategy has been updated multiple times), but he emphasizes that "net benefit" and "potential downside" are speculative until a change is released and observed in the wild. This is a pragmatic counterpoint to Phillip’s proposal: while the idea of weighing costs and benefits is sound in theory, measuring them in practice requires real-world data that the project currently lacks. Junio’s skepticism is not a rejection of the approach but a reminder of the limitations of pre-release analysis.

For the `core.defaults` proposal, Junio questions whether it would actually help measure the impact of default changes. He suggests that polling the value of `core.defaults` to gauge user adoption of "modern defaults" would be redundant, since the same information could be obtained by directly measuring the target configurations (e.g., how many users set `diff.algorithm` to a non-default value). His critique implies that `core.defaults` might add complexity without providing meaningful new insights. The example of multiplying the population of users who explicitly set `diff.algorithm` by three to estimate broader support is a rough heuristic, but it underscores Junio’s preference for direct measurement over indirect proxies.

The email does not propose an alternative or reject Phillip’s ideas outright, but it introduces a note of caution about the practical challenges of implementing them. Junio’s tone is measured and analytical, focusing on the mechanics of how such a system would work rather than its philosophical merits. This response is likely to prompt further discussion about how the project could gather the data needed to make informed decisions about default changes, whether through `core.defaults` or other means.

Key technical details remain unchanged: the `core.defaults` variable, the consequentialist approach, and the distinction between cosmetic and high-impact settings. Junio’s critique does not address the idea of workflow-specific default profiles (e.g., for triangular workflows), leaving that aspect of Phillip’s proposal open for further exploration. The email is substantive but narrow in scope, focusing on the operational challenges of Phillip’s ideas rather than their broader implications for Git’s evolution.
