This thread is about Jason Newton’s proposed worktree improvements—copy-on-write (CoW) creation via `--reflink` and automatic reaping of ephemeral branches—now in its third iteration (v3). The reflink feature, which avoids full checkout overhead on supported filesystems (Linux btrfs/XFS/bcachefs, macOS APFS), has been stripped of Windows ReFS support due to a silent corruption bug, leaving it permanently disabled there. The ephemeral branch reaping, designed to clean up auto-created branches when worktrees are removed, remains hotly contested as either a usability win or an unnecessary disruption.

The series has stalled amid sharp disagreements. Eric Sunshine and Phillip Wood argue the ephemeral branch feature is redundant with `--detach`, poorly justified, and too aggressive in its unconditional deletion of branches. Jason Newton defends it as a way to preserve branch-like semantics during a worktree’s lifetime, avoiding the "degraded halfway state" of detached HEADs. The reflink implementation is also contested: Eric Sunshine maintains it should default to automatic for tracked files, while Jason insists the opt-in design (via `--reflink` or config) is necessary to avoid copying untracked files. Junio’s early feedback suggested automatic reflink when available, but the current version remains configurable.

The thread has taken a combative turn, with Jason accusing reviewers of "nitpicking" and working in a "silo," while Eric and Phillip condemn his dismissive tone and lack of engagement with their core objections. The latest exchange—brian m. carlson’s procedural objection to Jason’s use of an AI-generated response—adds another layer of friction, framing it as a breach of mailing list norms. With no movement on the substantive issues and interpersonal tensions escalating, the series risks languishing unless the discussion can be refocused on clarifying the problem statement and addressing the technical concerns. For now, the reflink feature is technically sound but defaults to opt-in, and the ephemeral branch reaping remains a sticking point with no clear path to consensus.
